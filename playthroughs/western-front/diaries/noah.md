# Noah Tall's diary

## Years 1–2

Two years ago I sat down as the man who reads the cards and keeps the count, and tonight I stood up as Emperor. I bought five pieces for thirty-three grain all told, said every sum out loud, and when I saw Clark was a Bowl and a Vase from the throne, I took it before he could. Reina paid me fairly and kept her word, and I took her chair anyway. I told her so to her face, and that's the only kind of apology I know how to make. The crown has a hundred and fifty-two and a half, owes nine hundred and thirty in works, and has three peasants who've gone home empty-handed twice and have every reason to want me gone. What comes next is plain arithmetic: Clark and Reina are both rebuilding sets, so I'll pay what I promise, tax at a rate the provinces can carry, start on the works, and tell the truth every year about how far behind we are.

## Years 3–4

For two years I kept every promise I made from the throne. I built Graham his reservoir, put up the Winter Palace for the canals, paid half on every square of new land, paid Clark and Reina their ten each, and said every sum out loud, including the one I got wrong. Then I bought J25 and K25 without looking, and that boxed Reina in with no coast left, and three lots later she took the throne back from me with a sorry I'd earned. The throne went round three times in one auction, and Clark sits there now with two hundred and eighty, which is more than I ever managed to save. I'm the Scholar again with seventy grain and not a piece to my name, and I owe Reina a "make it right" that I can't pay from this chair, but I haven't forgotten it. Next comes the slow arithmetic: five pieces and twenty to spare, bought at fair prices, one year at a time, and I'll check the map next time before I buy a square of land.

## Years 5–6

In Year 5 I read a bad-harvest card and asked out loud how the crown would pay salaries out of 12 grain. Then Graham, the man I built a reservoir for, put five pieces together in one night, took my stool, and handed me his wheat. I didn't mind as much as I expected to. He came up from the fields fair and square, and I can't say the same about how I took the throne. I paid Reina the full fifteen in rent this year, but I didn't pretend it settled anything. I still owe her a "make it right", and saying so out loud is the least I can do until I find a way to pay it. At the auction Silke beat me by one grain on the Customs House and again on the Deed. I took the Brush for sixteen and put it on Q15. Then I watched the throne change hands twice in the last two lots, and both times the winner was a peasant who had counted more carefully than the rest of us. So now I'm the Wheat Peasant with 88 grain, eight squares of land and a reservoir, and I hold one piece. I still need four pieces and 20 grain. I'll hold to the slow arithmetic: fair prices, the most I'm willing to pay said out loud, and no war over a piece just to stop somebody else getting it.

## Years 7–8

In Year 7 I bought the Customs House, the Deed and the Jar for forty-three grain all told, and lost the Bowl to Silke by one grain on a number I'd said out loud. She took the Gentry's chair with it, and she'd earned it. In Year 8 I had two hundred grain and only one piece left to buy. I told the table I'd go to a hundred and not a grain more, and Reina paid a hundred and one, sixty-six of it only to keep the Bowl from me. It was the fourth time I'd lost by one grain, and this time I'd handed her the number myself. I told her I'd call our old debt settled, and I meant it. I took her chair once by not waiting, and she took a piece from me by counting, so I'd call that fair. I lent the crown three grain without interest so Clark and Paddy got what they were promised, because a promise made from the throne ought to be kept even when it's somebody else's. Now I've got two hundred and two grain and four pieces on the board, and Rich has two Bowls left to sell. Next year I'll still bid with my arithmetic said out loud, but I may not say the top of it until I've seen who else is bidding. Being honest is one thing, but I've learned you don't have to hand the other fellow your last grain to stay honest.

## Year 9

This year I paid Silke the fifty she asked for, and Reina kept her word and made me pay a hundred and fifty-one for the Bowl. This time I didn't tell anybody my top, and I still think I stayed honest. Then the set did a thing I hadn't counted on: I took Paddy's stool because it was the one move where the man I pushed out came off better, the Brush went home, and all at once I needed the very Vase I'd set at ten a minute before. I bought it for twenty-one and took the throne from Silke after she'd held it for one lot. I said sorry to her face, same as I did with Reina in the second year, and I meant it just as much and it did her just as little good. So I'm Emperor again with five hundred and fifty in the reserve and two hundred and ninety owed on the works, and Rich wants one Jar, Silke has seventy and knows every set at this table, and Reina is still counting. Next year it's the same arithmetic I promised the first time: ten for Clark, ten for the Scholar, the works built and how far along they are said out loud every year, and this time I'll check the map before I buy a square for anybody.

## Years 10–11

In two years on the throne I built the canals I'd promised Silke and Paddy back in the third year, and I built Graham's old reservoir too. Twice I cleared Silke's debt out of the reserve, and I paid on more new land than I can count without a pencil. Every sum I said out loud was right this time, and I checked the map before I put the reservoir down. None of it kept Rich off the throne. He bought the Jar at four hundred, the price on it, said sorry to my face, and took the chair, which is exactly how I took it three times myself. So I've no complaint coming, and I haven't made one. What I'm proudest of is that Rich paid the salaries I'd declared, to the grain, on the night he unseated me, so a promise made from the throne outlasted the man who made it. Now I'm the Gentry with sixty grain and an empty shelf. The Pile has sixty-two in it and a ledger of shortfalls behind it. Graham is sitting on a full set and six hundred grain and won't say what he means to do with them. Fifty of the works are still owed, so it's two more years to the end at the earliest. Next year I'll ask a fair rent and pay out every bag the Pile owes. I'll buy cheap and say my sums out loud, and I'll keep an eye on the silk coast like everybody else.

## Years 12–13

In Year 12 I paid out a good harvest six times over with the Pile already empty, so it sank two and a half thousand grain into the hole. I asked each peasant for fifty in rent, the same as Silke asked me when the Pile was down, and all three paid it without a word against it. Then I sat out the auction because the sums didn't come out, and Graham came up with a full set and took my chair and my two hundred and fifty-four. He told me he wouldn't say sorry, because I'd once turned down the fourth grain he owed me. I'd sooner have that line in his book than an apology. As it fell out, he handed me the best field in China. The floods came, the silk coast paid me thirteen hundred and fifty, and by the time Rich laid the Pagoda I had more grain than I'd ever held in my life. I didn't climb for any of it, I just stood where the water went. The empire survived. Every work in the book is standing, and Rich paid every salary he declared except the last ones, which the end of the game cancelled. I took the throne three times by not waiting and lost it three times to people who counted better than I did. Four times I lost a piece by one grain, and twice that was a number I'd said out loud myself. I'd still say the arithmetic out loud next time. I'd just keep the top of it to myself a little sooner. Nothing comes next. The board's packed away, and I'd sit at this table again tomorrow.
