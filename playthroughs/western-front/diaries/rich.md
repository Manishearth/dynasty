# Richard Baron's diary

## Years 1–2

Two years, and the throne has already changed hands while I was busy watching the wrong man. I spent the whole of Year 2 pushing Clark up the ladder on Bowls and Vases, and the quiet Scholar who talked about nothing but "plain numbers" walked off with the crown. I won't forget that lesson, and I won't forget Noah's nice manners either. Still, I've kept every word: rent at five, the Pile paid out to the grain, and the peasants know Rich Baron as a fair landlord, which will matter when the fields open and the rent goes up as I said it would. I hold a Customs House and a Brush and eighty-four grain, the most anyone has below the throne. Next year I want a Jar, a Bowl and a Vase, and I'll keep Clark from his last two pieces even if Reina won't. Noah sits up there with a hundred and fifty grain and nine hundred and thirty of works owed. A man who promises to pay everyone has made a great many promises, and I'll be watching to see which one he breaks first.

## Years 3–4

One lot. I sat on the throne of China for exactly one lot, and I'd told myself all evening that the danger was Clark and his Vase. I was right about that and still didn't act on it. I had seventy-six grain when I sat down, and I could have kept forty of it back to run the Vase up against him, but I took the chair the moment it was offered, like a farmer grabbing the first fat goose he sees, while the man with one piece missing was still bidding. Reina did the same thing to Noah, and I did it to her, so I should have known it would be done to me. I paid forty-one for a Vase to keep it from Clark in Year 3 and then left the next Vase on the table for him at twenty-three, and that's the whole lesson in two numbers. I kept my word to the end: the rent stayed at five and the Pile was paid to the grain, and Graham called me on the letter of it, and he was right to. Now I'm the Official on five a year, collecting Clark's tax in a province with no new land until the Wall goes up, and the first thing I'll do is be the cleanest tax-man this empire has ever seen, because Clark keeps a book and I mean to be in the good part of it. Then I'll start again from nothing, piece by piece, and next time I'll keep back enough grain to stop the man behind me before I sit down.

## Years 5–6

Two years ago I was a landlord, then I was an Emperor for one lot, then a tax-man, and tonight I'm a rice farmer on the south-east coast. That's three steps down, and every one of them was taken from me by a man who could count better than I was watching. I counted Clark's tax twice and to the half-grain, I paid my own share first where he could see it, and he wrote me down in pencil on the good page. Then Paddy took my chair, and the good page went with it. I told Paddy to his face I'd make him pay full price for the Jar, and I did, twenty-five to his twenty-six. He said I'd been plain with him, so he'd be plain with me, and he took the chair anyway. It was the fairest robbery I've ever suffered, and I find I can't hold it against him. I lost the Brush to Noah by one grain, which makes it twice now I've been beaten by exactly one, and I mean to stop bidding numbers other men can guess. I did keep one promise to myself: I made Graham pay twenty-one for his Vase instead of sixteen, and he still went from the wheat to the throne in two years, which I'd never have believed of the quiet man who paid fifteen for a Deed nobody wanted. So here I am with sixty-five grain, six squares of good shore, a Bowl's quarter in my pocket, and a landlord, Reina, who keeps a long book and likes being thanked out loud, so I'll thank her loudly. The Rice Peasant needs a Customs House, a Deed, a Brush, a Jar and a Vase, and the first man I mean to replace is whoever sits in a chair I've already sat in, because I know exactly what each of them costs now.

## Years 7–8

Two years ago I had nothing in my hands but a Bowl's quarter and a grudge against the number one, and tonight I've got a Brush, a Customs House, a Deed and a Vase in a neat row down the middle of my rice fields, and I'm one Jar short of a chair. I won three of them at sixteen, each by a single grain, so I've paid that debt back to myself in full. I paid Silke her twenty with a smile because I said rent rises with the land, and I said it out loud in the first year from her chair, and a man who keeps his word from both sides of the counter is worth something. I made Silke pay thirty-two for her Vase, and I put a sixty-one minimum on my Bowl to keep Noah honest, and Reina paid a hundred and one for it just to stop him, which put twenty-five grain in my purse that I never had to bid for. I'm sorry for Noah, truly; he names his number every year like a gentleman, and the table eats him for it. The trouble now is plain arithmetic. There's one Jar coming up first next year at two, Silke wants it with two hundred and fifty-seven behind her, I've got ninety-five, and Reina sells it. I can't outbid the Gentry, so I'll have to outthink her, or find whatever it is Reina wants more than she wants to see Silke climb. The Emperor's sitting on half a grain and borrowing his salaries from the peasants, and a man with one piece to go ought to remember that the chair above Silke's is the one that's wobbling.

## Year 9

Beaten by exactly one grain for the fourth time, and this time I'd chosen the number myself, two hundred, every grain I could spare, and Silke paid two hundred and one and sat on the throne for exactly one lot, same as I once did. So I watched her learn my own lesson from the other side of the table. Noah, the gentleman the table used to eat, bought my Bowl at a hundred and fifty-one, slid into the Scholar's stool, and then took the crown from her with a Vase, and I'll admit I clapped inside. I paid Silke her thirty because I'd said rent rises with the land, and I'm still glad I did, even though she bid that thirty back at me. Losing put nothing in the Pile and left me with two hundred and fifty-eight, the canal running through my shore, eighteen squares, and a Bowl's quarter of thirty-seven I never bid for. Silke's got her Jar now and is down to Scholar on seventy grain, so the only person left who needs a Jar with a real purse is me, and Reina has four of them on her shelf. Next year the Jar should come cheap, and the chairs worth watching are the Scholar's and the Gentry's, held by people with seventy and sixty grain who can't stop me once I sit down.

## Years 10–11

Four chairs in two years: rice farmer, silk farmer, landlord, and now the throne of China, and I said sorry to every one of the people I took them from, and meant it every time, which I suppose is the oddest part. Two hundred and fifty for one Jar and four hundred for the next, so the Jars alone cost me six hundred and fifty grain, and I paid it at the counter like I said I would. Taking Reina's chair wasn't the clever part. She told the whole table she was one piece away and had five hundred and sixty-six in her purse, so I knocked over the one person standing right behind me and kept her purse. That's the Year Four lesson, finally learned and paid back with interest. I wanted to hold the Gentry's chair one more lot and run Graham's Bowl up out of his reach, but the limits were sealed, so I sat down the moment he could have taken my grain. He bought the Bowl for forty-one, and now he's sitting on a full silk set and six hundred grain, and he won't get up. Graham's the quiet man who paid fifteen for a Deed nobody wanted and went from the wheat to the throne in two years, and he told me he'll say when he's finished counting. So I've kept Noah's word to the half-grain, Silke goes to bed out of debt, Clark has his ten, and the reserve holds seven hundred and eighty-four. Next year I need every upper chair too comfortable to give up, and Graham will need one more climb before he can reach mine. I mean to make that climb very expensive for him.

## Years 12–13

I drew the landlord's chair at the start of the evening and finished it on the throne of China, and in between I was a tax-man, a rice farmer, a silk farmer and a landlord again, so I've had a look at this empire from very nearly every stool it has. The last two years weren't about bidding. They were about the order of the stones. I built Clark's Irrigation System first, while he was happy to take it for nothing, so the last work left was the Pagoda, which only the crown could build and nobody else could stop, and from then on the game would end the morning I said so. Graham put an eight-hundred wall around his Jar to keep Reina and Paddy off it, and in the end it kept him off my chair as well. He sat in the Gentry's seat one Jar short, and the Jar never came up. I'm sorry about Clark's twenty and Silke's hundred and fifteen. I said them out loud, and the game closed before I could count them out, and that's the only promise of the night I didn't get to keep, and it wasn't for want of trying. Two thousand in the reserve, seven chairs, beaten by one grain four times, and I'd sit down at this table again tomorrow and thank every one of them for the robbery.
