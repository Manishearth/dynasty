# Reina Osorio's diary

## Years 1–2

Two years on the throne, and I spent them building: the Summer Palace, a canal through my own province, and double salaries for my Official and my Scholar so they'd stay loyal. I was watching the tax rate and the revolt clause and never once looked at the auction table, and while I wasn't looking, Noah quietly bought himself a full set and took the crown along with the reserve I'd saved. He did say sorry, and he paid me my ten. I'll give him that, and I'll remember it. Now I'm the Scholar with forty grain, a Bowl and a Vase, and the Bowl and Vase cost me thirty mostly to stop Clark finishing his own set and taking a seat from one of us. I need a Customs House, a Land Deed and a Dragon Jar, and I'll be watching Rich's Customs House and Silke's jars. From now on I watch the shelves, not just the granary.

## Years 3–4

Year Three I bid fourteen a piece and came home with nothing, while Clark and Rich spent forty apiece and ended one piece short each. I should have seen then that the shelves were about to tip. Year Four I bought the Customs House, the Deed and the Jar in a row, took the throne back from Noah, and said sorry to his face. Then I sat in that chair for one lot. Rich bought a Bowl and threw me out, and Clark bought a Vase and threw him out. The man who keeps the book now wears the crown, and he's sitting on two hundred and eighty grain of reserves I'd saved twice over. I'm the Gentry now, with sixty grain, nothing on my shelf, and a province under an unbuilt Wall, so I can't buy land until Clark builds it. What I learned: having a set isn't safe while two other people are one piece from theirs. I won too early, or I didn't block enough. But I run the Harvest Pile and the rent book now, so every peasant at this table deals with me once a year, and I'll be generous with them and keep count. Clark owes me nothing yet. He'll owe me a Wall.

## Years 5–6

Two years, and I bought nothing at the auction table. I kept my word to myself: I bid small, I paid the Harvest Pile nothing, and I got my Wall. Ten grain the first year and twenty the second, Clark's stones with my name on them, and G9 and G10 planted underneath. I'm a landlord with land at last, earning thirty-five in an average year instead of losing five. Then I sat and watched three chairs change hands in a single round. Paddy finished the rice set and took Rich's seat, a Bowl took Clark's throne, and a Vase handed it to Graham, all inside one auction. My keep-from on the Jar never mattered, because Rich was the one who fed it. Clark is the Official now with nothing, so the man who built my Wall has no Wall left to build, and I owe him and will remember it. Silke paid me five when I asked fifteen and told me exactly why, and I respect that more than I like it. Noah paid the full fifteen and told me the old debt is still open, so his name goes in the good column. Graham is on the throne now, a peasant three years ago, with two hundred and fifty in the reserve and a book he says he'll keep in ink. Next comes land before the counter doubles, rent that tracks the harvest, and a cheap Brush while there are any left. I've learned to watch the shelves, and now I'm learning to wait.

## Years 7–8

In Year Seven I took Graham at his word and bought four squares at five grain each, the cheapest land I'll ever own. I watched Silke and told the table she was two pieces from a chair. I set my keep-froms on the Bowl and the Vase at fifteen, and she paid twenty-nine and thirty-two, and then she took my seat. She took it fairly and she'd said she would, so I'm not angry, I'm only tired of learning the same lesson. Then the coast she gave back turned out to be the richest field in China. I paid her ten in rent, exactly what she paid me, and bought eight squares of sea. I'd gone from fifty grain to nearly three hundred in a single harvest. In Year Eight I spent a hundred and one on a wooden bowl to keep Noah a peasant, and he called his old debt to me settled, which I didn't expect and will miss a little. Clark beat me to the last Brush by a grain, so I can never finish a set unless one comes back to the shelves. That makes me a seller now, not a buyer. The Jar I priced at three hundred has to go up at two next year, and Silke and Rich each need only that Jar. Whoever buys it takes a chair, and unless I hold the auction right, the chair goes to whoever has the most grain left. Next year I'll need to talk before I bid: work out which of them I'd rather see climb, and what that's worth to me in public.

## Year 9

Year Nine I watched three thrones change hands in a single auction, and none of them was mine to take or to lose. Silke kept her word to the letter: she bought my Jar for two hundred and one, took Graham's chair and not mine, and then sat on it for one lot, the same lot-and-a-half lesson I had carved for myself in Year Four. My keep-from made Noah pay a hundred and fifty-one for a wooden bowl, and it still wasn't enough. I'd forgotten that the Scholar collects the Vase, he had, and I stopped one grain short on it at twenty, the same single grain that beat me to the Brush last year. So Noah sits on the throne for the third time, with five hundred and fifty in the reserve, and he says sorry to everyone he passes on the way up, and somehow it still works. What it cost me is mostly pride: three grain for a Customs House, thirty in rent, a hundred on ten squares of sea, and fifty-two of my Jar money. I end the year with three hundred and fifty-seven, a Bowl and a Customs House, the Deed opening at two next year, Brushes back on the shelf, and a coastline that pays seven hundred in an average year. For the first time a set is within reach if I buy the cheap pieces before anyone notices I'm buying them. Rich needs only a Jar and I hold four of them, so the next time a Jar goes on the counter I decide who climbs. Graham collects the rent now, a man who's just lost a throne, so I'll pay him fairly and see whether he remembers who did.

## Years 10–11

Year Ten I had the richest coast in China, eight hundred grain and four pieces in a tidy row, and I told the whole table I was one Vase short because I thought honesty would keep me safe. Rich listened, paid my two hundred and fifty for the Jar, said sorry, and took the coast, the grain and the chair all at once. So I've now been unseated for being honest as many times as for being careless, and I can't decide which lesson stings more. I paid half my own land to spare the reserve, and the reserve went to Rich anyway. In Year Eleven I began again as a rice farmer with sixty grain, paid my rent, took three cheap squares, and bid thirty a piece while Graham bid one grain more on three of them and walked off with a whole set. Now he sits on it with six hundred grain, not getting up, the most dangerous man at the table, and Rich is on the throne with Noah's reserve and the habit of saying sorry. I have three hundred and thirty-six, a Vase tucked in a corner where nobody's counting, and a shore that pays four hundred and fifty a year. Next year I keep quiet, buy cheap and spread out, and let everyone keep their eyes on Graham. If he climbs, I want to be the one he passes over, not the one he takes.

## Years 12–13

In Year Twelve I did what I promised myself. I kept quiet, set my pieces in different corners, and outbid Paddy by a single grain twice, the same grain that had beaten me so many times before. The Customs House, the Deed and the Brush cost me a hundred and twenty-five, and I ended the year one Jar from a chair, sure that it would open at two the next spring. I'd forgotten the other clock: Clark warned us out loud that only one Pagoda was left, and Rich told us he had the mortar ready. He built it before any Jar went on the counter, so the game ended with Graham and me sitting side by side, each one Jar short. I drew the throne at the start of the evening and I finish it in the rice fields with eight hundred and ninety-two and a half grain. Somehow that's more than I ever held as Empress, though it's still a long way below the seat I started in. I planned years ahead all night, and in the end I lost to the one year I didn't count. Next time I'll count the works as carefully as I count the shelves, and I'll buy my pieces the year before the last stone goes up, not the year it does.
