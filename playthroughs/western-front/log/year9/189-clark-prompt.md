## Clark's diary so far, in his own words

## Years 1–2

Years One and Two, entered in the other book. I collected every grain of tax honestly, I picked up a Deed, a Brush and a Jar cheaply on the first night, and I thought I was the cleverest man at the table, right up until the Scholar quietly finished his own set and walked off with the throne. I warned the whole table out loud that I wanted the Bowl and the Vase. That was stupid. It gave Reina the cue to outbid me by one grain on each, so I go into Year Three still two pieces short, with seventy-four grain and a fresh grudge against her. There are three entries under her name now: the Bowl, the Vase, and that smile. Noah kept his word and paid me ten, so he goes in the good book for now. But he has the crown, and I have the tax, and from here on I mean to remind him how much he depends on the man who collects it. Next year I keep my mouth shut at the auction, I bid like a man with grain to spare, and I take the Bowl and the Vase before anyone has time to count.

## Years 3–4

Years Three and Four, and this time the good book got an entry under my own name. I kept my mouth shut in Year Three, took the Bowl at thirty-six, and then watched Rich go to forty-one on the Vase and call it sentimentality. That went straight into the other book, under Reina's three entries. In Year Four I was five grain in the red every year with no land I was allowed to buy. I collected the crown's tax anyway and told Noah to his face that his collector was starving under an unbuilt Wall. Then came the auction where three people sat on the throne in three lots: Reina for a lot and a half, Rich for one, and me last, with the Vase for twenty-three because Graham kept his word and Noah wouldn't go past twenty-two. I paid Noah ten because he paid me ten when I was nobody, and I paid Rich five because he hasn't earned more yet. Now I have two hundred and eighty in the reserve, eight hundred and fifty owed on the works, a Wall that needs building in a province that isn't mine any more, and six people who have all watched how quickly this chair changes hands. Next year I buy land for the provinces that will pay tax on it, I watch every piece that goes up for sale, and I keep both books open, because the man in this chair can't afford to forget anything.

## Years 5–6

Years Five and Six, and the good book closed both years on a page about my own foolishness. In Year Five I emptied the treasury into everyone else's fields, fifteen grain a square, seventy for Reina's Wall, and ended the year with two grain. While I was paying for their seed, Graham bought five pieces at the wheat-price and walked off with the Scholar's chair. In Year Six I taxed properly, finished Reina's Wall and gave Rich his ten, and then Paddy, who told me once he'd be no enemy of mine over a Bowl, bought a Bowl for twenty-two and took the throne. He sat in it for exactly one lot before Graham took it from him. So I'm the Official now, with seventy grain, not one piece on the shelf, and an unbuilt Wall over my head, collecting tax for a man I gave a Reservoir and fifteen grain a square. Paddy Doyle has a fresh page in the other book. Graham has one too, though he kept my salary promise to the grain, so his name goes in the good book in pencil. Next time I don't spend a grain on anyone who can count to five pieces. I count the tax honestly, I make Graham build the Wall above me, and I collect the five pieces quietly, the way the peasants did, because it turns out this chair goes to whoever is patient, not whoever is generous.

## Years 7–8

Years Seven and Eight, and the good book finally has an entry in ink. In Year Seven I started with an empty shelf. I lost every lot I bid on, watched Silke buy the Bowl and the Vase and take Reina's chair, and then lent the crown twelve grain so it could pay me ten of it. Graham paid back fourteen off the top, as promised, and built all four links over my head, so his name moves out of pencil. In Year Eight I bought my five squares at half, which takes me from minus forty-five a year to plus fifty-five, and I took the last Bamboo Brush in China for thirteen, one grain over Reina. That's the first single grain at this table that's ever gone my way. It cost me nearly everything, and I end the year with twenty-eight grain and one piece on J12, where my last set started. Reina gets a new entry in the other book, but it's an admiring one: three hundred on a Jar and a hundred and one on a Bowl just to keep two people out of chairs. Silke and Rich both need only a Jar, the Jar reopens at two first thing next year, and whichever of them gets it will start a scramble. Next year I keep collecting honestly, I let the fields fill my granary, I buy the cheap pieces nobody's watching, and I sit on the only Brush in the country until somebody has to come and ask me for it.
