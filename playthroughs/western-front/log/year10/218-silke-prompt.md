## Silke's diary so far, in her own words

## Years 1–2

Two years of auctions and I have nothing on my shelf. I lost twice to Rich on ties at eight, just because his seat comes before mine, so a bid that only matches his is worth nothing to me. Noah went from preaching small bids to sitting on the throne inside one evening, and Reina paid fifteen apiece just to keep the Bowl and Vase from Clark. That's how the people at the top really play: they bid what a piece is worth to them, and nobody stops to work out what's fair. My Jars did earn their keep. Five went to Clark, nine to Noah, and the nine has gone back into stock, so they'll be wanted again. Paddy and Graham and I are still sitting at fifty-nine to sixty-one with empty hands. Next year I bid odd numbers, and above whatever Rich is willing to pay, on the pieces I actually need. I'll keep the Jar price high, because Reina and Rich both need one now. I'm being squeezed, and I've learned more watching these two years than I would have by winning.

## Years 3–4

Four years and my shelf is still empty, and this time I picked it that way. I put forty grain into three fields on the coast while the crown was paying half. That left me too thin to fight Graham for the last Brush, and he got it at twenty-four. Then I sat and watched the throne change hands three times in one auction: Reina, then Rich, then Clark, each of them saying sorry and none of them meaning it. What I learned is that a finished set gets you a chair, and the person sitting in it only keeps it until the next lot is sold. My Jar went to Reina for eighteen, and I kept four and a half of it. Those three fields pay sixty a year from now on, and every piece I need has gone back to stock, the Brushes included, so the market I was shut out of is open again. Clark has two hundred and eighty in the treasury, Paddy is down to thirty-six and a half, and Graham has spent his purse on a Deed and a Brush. Next year I'll have the steadiest income of any peasant at this table, and when the population doubles again I won't be the one going hungry.

## Years 5–6

Two years ago my shelf was empty. Now I have a Customs House, a Deed and a Brush in a row down the middle of the coast, and every one of them cost me an odd number. Seven new squares went in while the crown was paying most of the price, and this year's harvest proved they were right: a hundred and sixty in, sixty-four out to Rich's count, and only five to Reina, because she charged me five in the lean year and I don't forget either. I lost the Bowl to Paddy by one grain, at twenty-one to his twenty-two, and an hour later Graham, who was a wheat farmer the year before, sat on the throne. Paddy climbed past Rich, Graham climbed past Paddy, and Clark has gone back to counting tax for whoever's sitting in the chair. I set the Jar at twenty-one to make Paddy pay for his chair, and it cost him twenty-six, and the first thing he did with the chair was lose it. What it cost me is that I'm still a peasant, two pieces short. But the Bowl and the Vase I need have both just gone back to stock, I have eighty-one grain and the biggest harvest of any peasant at this table, and every one of them has learned that the chair goes to whoever finishes the set, not whoever has been waiting longest. Next year I finish mine.

## Years 7–8

Six years on the coast, and in the seventh I finished the set with one grain to spare on each of the last two pieces, twenty-nine for the Bowl and thirty-two for the Vase, both times one over the man behind me. Then I took Reina's chair, the one I told the camera in the third year I could take. It cost me twenty fields that pay four hundred in a good year, and I handed them straight to the woman I took the chair from. I knew that would happen. I didn't expect her to learn so quickly what to do with them. She set the Jar at three hundred, which is my own lesson from six years behind the counter, and then she spent sixty-six grain just to keep Noah from his Bowl. So now there are two of us one Jar short, Rich and me, and every Jar in China sits with Reina. What she can't do is keep it high: the Jar goes up first next year at two, and I have two hundred and fifty-seven grain against Rich's ninety-five and a half. Graham's treasury is down to half a grain and he's borrowing salaries from the table. The rule says a Gentry with a full set can take the throne. Next year I buy the Jar, and then I take the throne.

## Year 9

I said the eighth year and I did it in the ninth: two hundred and one for the Jar, one grain over Rich, and the throne was mine. I held it for exactly one lot. Noah paid a hundred and fifty-one for a Bowl and twenty-one for a Vase and walked up through Paddy's stool to my chair. He said sorry to my face, which is more than Reina, Rich or Clark ever managed, and I can't fault him for it, because it's the same lesson I taught Reina. Whoever waits loses the throne. It cost me everything I'd built: twenty fields given to Reina last year, five pieces back into stock this year, and my province's harvest handed to whoever sits in the Gentry's chair. What I have now is seventy grain, the Scholar's stool, an empty shelf, and a card I turned over myself that doubles the mouths on my table to six hundred million. Graham is running my old Harvest Pile with sixty grain, Rich still needs only one Jar, and Noah has five hundred and fifty in the reserve. Next year I start again from nothing, which is where I started this game, and back then I learned that losing teaches more than winning, so I'll be counting every grain at this table the way I did when I was squeezed the first time.
