# Silke's diary

## Setup–Year 2

Silke Weber, diary, end of Year 2

I hold the bank, the Scroll and the only Wooden Bowl in China. Two years ago I'd have called that a fine position. Today I've got twenty-seven and a half grain, and I have to finish on twenty. The Bowl cost me twenty-five, because Graham made me pay for every grain of it. He did me a favour, though: now I know exactly how far he'll go for something he can't do without.

I kept my word on the Scroll and stood aside for Paddy. I want that remembered, because Paddy's last Brush is the next thing I need. He no longer owes me it at the minimum, so I'll have to ask him nicely.

Graham holds the Vase, and I hold the last Customs House he needs. Next year we'll find out whether he wants a fair trade or a second war, and I can't afford a second war. Rob's legs heal soon. He's had a soldier standing on O19 since Year 1 and has already taken five from me for the view. I've kept everything deep on the coast at O22 and O23, out of his reach. Reina writes everything down. So do I now.

## Years 3–4

Silke Weber, diary, Year 4

Last year I had the Scroll, the Bowl and the Brush all lined up on the coast, and I needed only the Vase. Paddy beat me to it by one grain, twenty-nine to my twenty-eight, then took my province and my fifty grain and left me his rice fields. He was right about one thing: me standing aside on his Scroll cost me nothing. I'll remember that the next time I think a favour like that has bought me anything.

I started again from nothing. Graham took the Jar off me for twenty-three because he's got more grain than I have, and I'll never have more grain than Graham. What I do have is the only Wooden Bowl in China. I held it back a year, and within a minute he'd agreed to let me have his Scroll at two. That's how you make a deal, so I'll be keeping the Bowl until it's paid for itself.

Paddy now says he'll stay out of the Bowl, and Graham has promised the table he'll keep his word. Next year I sell the Bowl at two, as I said I would, and Graham finishes his set. Then I'll find out whose seat he wants and whose grain comes with it. Rob's soldier is one square from Graham's shop window, and my Scroll is on Q25, the only corner of this province he can't reach. From now on I put up what I hold only when it buys me something.

## Years 5–6

Silke Weber, diary, end of Year 6

I sold the Bowl at two, as I said I would. Paddy kept his promise to stay out of it for exactly one year, then offered Graham forty for it the next morning. That's the trouble with promises that come with dates on them. I paid Rob twenty for a soldier to guard my Scroll, and Rob marched straight over him and took both. So I paid the Warlord to protect me from the Warlord, and I won't do that again.

Then Graham bought his Brush back from Rob for thirty, finished his set, and took the silk coast from Paddy rather than from me. He said it was because I'd kept every promise I made him. That's the first favour in six years that actually paid me back, and it only cost me two grain at an auction. Rob finished his set too and walked into the Winter Palace with Reina's book under his arm, and now Reina is out in the desert feeding his soldiers.

I've got twenty-eight grain and nothing on the board, which is worse than I started. But every piece in China is back up for auction, nobody holds a set, and I hold the bank and the only Brush in the Rice stock. Nobody at this table can afford to have me as an enemy now, because I've got nothing left to lose. This time I don't pay anyone for protection. I sell them what they need, and I sell it dear.

## Years 7–8

Silke Weber, diary, Year 8

The famine took my twenty-eight and left me thirty-two in debt, and with debt my hands were tied. I couldn't bid, buy or trade. All I could do was sell. I put Paddy's old Brush up at fifteen so nobody could buy it just to keep it from him, and nobody did, so I said I'd sell it dear and then didn't sell it at all. Rob finished my canal with his own grain, the first thing he ever paid for, and the next card took half of what it earned. Then Graham, one Vase short of the throne with exactly twenty in hand, watched the weather sell his whole set back to the pile at two a piece. By the second lot of Year 8 every seat at the table was in the red and I was the furthest down at sixty-seven, so I did the one thing a banker can do when the sum never comes right: I closed the books. I proposed we give up, and all five of us said yes, Graham last. Nobody won, but nobody won on my grain either, and every one of them kept their word to me at the very end. After eight years I'll take that.
