# Graham Garner's diary

## Years 1–2

Year one cost me fourteen grain in losing bids and bought me nothing. Paddy offered me the Customs House cheap and then outbid me by a grain, and Silke did the same with the Brush. Year two put it right: the Customs House and the Brush came to me at four each, and I'm writing down that Paddy kept his word the second time, as well as that he didn't the first. The Reservoir is a fine thing, twenty a year that I never had to bid for, and it's the reason I'm sitting on 91 and a half. Now every one of us holds the only copy of a piece the other two need. Silke has the Jar, Paddy has the Bowl and I have the Vase, so next year's auctions are where the set gets decided. I'll pay what it takes for the Jar and the Bowl, I won't sell the Vase cheap, and I'll keep my grain dry until then.

## Years 3–4

Year three went exactly as I'd worked it out. The Jar at forty-six and the Bowl at thirty-nine, both under my limits, and the set was finished. I took Silke's seat and her grain, 190 in all, and told her why to her face. I don't regret it. In year four I bought her Deed at sixty, which was a fair price, and sold Paddy the Jar at sixty, which was the mistake. I knew what a finished set would let him do. I said it to the camera before the auction and then I sold it to him anyway, because sixty looked like a good number and fifteen of it was mine. Fifteen grain. He took 183 and a half off me with it, and the Deed, and the Brush, and the Bowl, and the seat that leads to the throne. He said he wouldn't forget that I didn't fight him. I'll be precise about this: he shook my hand in year three and sat in my chair in year four. I have 65 grain, two canal sections in Province 6, and nothing on the board. Province 6 sells the Brush and the Bowl, and those are the two pieces Paddy needs for the throne. He'll pay what I ask for them, or he'll wait, and every year he waits the rest of us get closer. I've started counting again from nothing, and this time I'm counting Paddy as well.

## Years 5–6

Year five I bought three fields on the crown's promise of ten a square and got less than five. Reina paid the fifteen and a half back the next spring, first thing, as she'd said, and I've crossed it out. Paddy paid seventy for the Brush and a hundred and twenty for the Bowl without a word of haggling. That's a hundred grain to me from the man who took a hundred and eighty-three off me, so I've got some of it back, if not all, and he took the throne instead of my seat, which I'll write down to his credit. I lost the Jar to Silke at fifty-three. She paid every grain she had for it to settle Year One, and I let her, because I had nothing left to answer with. Now I've a hundred and nine and a half, five fields on the coast, a Deed on R22, and the Brush and the Bowl back in my stock. Silke holds everything but the Wooden Bowl, and I'm the only one who sells it. She has twenty-nine grain, and the nearest seat she could take is mine. So the Bowl won't go up next year. The Brush will, and Reina needs it. The Bowl waits until I've counted exactly what Silke would do with it, and what it would cost me.

## Years 7–8

Year seven I bought the Customs House at twenty-five and four squares at ten each, and I stopped at forty for the Vase because I'd said forty, and Reina took it at forty-one. Year eight I had two hundred and forty grain and a Bowl that had to go up, and I counted Reina. I paid ninety for a Deed I didn't need so she couldn't finish, and I never once counted Silke, who had the Brush, the Customs House and the Jar on her own squares and needed one piece, and that piece was mine. I wrote last year that the Bowl would wait until I'd counted exactly what Silke would do with it. I didn't count it. She paid me a hundred and eleven for it and then took two hundred and sixty-seven and a half off me with it, and she told me why to my face, the way I told her in year three. I'll write that down fairly: she was owed it. I have sixty-five grain, her desert, which she bought well, and nothing in my hand. Only the Pagoda is left to build, and it's twenty grain, so the game could end next spring before I've set out a single piece. I've started counting again from nothing, the second time now. This time I'm going to count every one of them, not just the one I'm watching.

## Year 9

Year nine was the last spring, and I never bought a square or set out a piece in it. An average harvest, two grain of charity, thirty percent to the crown, and Paddy laid the Pagoda between his two palaces for twenty grain. That was the last of the works, and the game ended there. I finish on two hundred and seventeen and a half, in the Wheat seat I drew, so it goes down as a hold. That's the right word for it, because I held it twice and lost it twice, and I'm only sitting in it now because Silke put me back. Silke has five hundred and seventy and Paddy's crown has four hundred and fifty-four. The one with the most is the one I never counted, and the one on the throne is the one who sat in my chair in year four. It cost me the Jar at sixty, the Bowl at a hundred and eleven, and ninety for a Deed I didn't need, and every one of those prices looked fair when I named it. Paddy said "an awful lot of fair dealing," and he's right, it was all fair. My trouble was never the price. I sold to whoever paid it without asking what they'd do with the piece. Nothing comes next for this table, because the dynasty survived and the works are finished. I'm glad of that. I said at the start I'd rather we finished the works than squabbled, and we did. If there's another table I'll do what I've written twice and done once: count all of them, every year, before I take a single grain for anything.
