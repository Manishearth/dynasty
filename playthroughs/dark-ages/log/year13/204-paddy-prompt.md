## Paddy's diary so far, in his own words

Two years in, and somehow I'm the one holding a full set: the House, the Deed and the Jar, all down by the sea for twenty-one grain. Last year I paid thirty in tax and lost the Deed on a tie. This year the whole table stood up against fifty percent, me with them, and Reina answered with a promise and a threat instead of a cut, so now there's no Emperor and no tax, and nothing gets built either. I didn't expect to feel uneasy about that, but I do. Rob's soldier is already standing in Province 6 and took five off me tonight, and with nobody on the throne there's nobody to stop him. Clark has promised me the first canal in writing, Rich is suddenly very friendly, and Rob says he'll keep his swords off whoever backs him. I'll back none of them on a promise. Whoever gets my name will have to show me they'll actually dig the canal, and I'll keep my grain close until then.

## Years 3–4

Two fat years with no tax, and I've more grain than I've ever had, a hundred and forty and more. You'd think I'd be pleased. Then Rob's lad walked into S24 and carried my Deed off to the desert, and there was nobody on the throne to complain to, so my set's broken again and his soldier is standing right next to my House and my Jar. Rich offered me twenty-five for the Jar, very politely, but that Jar's the only thing keeping his set from my seat, so I told him no. Clark has finally put the first canal for Province 6 into the record, and I'll grant him that it's more than anyone else has given me, but he's still only on paper and I've watched him count before. I'm keeping my name back for now. No chair means no new land and no canal, and one bad harvest with Rob's swords about would cost us more than any tax did, so before long somebody will have to be crowned. When it comes to it I'll back whoever will actually build, and in the meantime I need to find myself another Deed.

## Years 5–6

Rob's lad walked off with my Jar in the drought, and that was the end of my patience with an empty chair. My set was gone, every mouth on the coast was eating twice, and there was land to be had that nobody could buy. So when Clark finally put Province 6's canal into the record with a year beside it, I put my name on him, and by the end of the afternoon the whole table had done the same, even Rich and Rob. He's kept the first few lines already: five new squares down the coast with the crown paying seventy of the hundred, and the twenty for my soldier. Then Rob's man stepped onto S24 and captured that soldier in the same year I bought it, so my shore is back to answering with nothing. I bid thirty-five for Silke's Jar mostly to keep it from Rich, and he paid thirty-six, then lost his Bowl to Rob before supper, so at least nobody at the table is sitting on a set. I'm down to my Customs House and a hundred and fifty grain, but I've got land under me now and a canal promised for next year. I'll take Clark at his word until next year's count, and I'll watch every grain of it.

## Years 7–8

The canal's in. Both sections, Year 7, the way Clark wrote it down, and I stood on the bank and watched them dig it in a bad harvest. That's twenty more on my harvest every year, and I'll not forget who paid for it. The crown was nearly empty, so I let it owe me fifty on the land instead of going hungry, and Clark paid me back every grain the next spring. In all my years at this table I've never seen a debt come home that fast. Rob's lad paid my toll on the new canal like a gentleman and then walked off with my Customs House, so I've no pieces left at all now, only land, nine squares of it up the coast, and soil can't be carried to the desert. I'm richer than I've ever been and I hold nothing anyone could steal, and I'm surprised to find I like it that way. Next year it's the Wall over the north, and Reina has the date in ink. I'll pay my tax and I'll watch the count, and if the crown keeps that figure the way it kept mine, I'll keep my name on Clark.

## Years 9–10

Two years, and the Wall stands from Province 1 to the desert, built in Year 9 and Year 10 the way Clark wrote it down in Year 8. It doesn't cover my roof, and I paid a hundred and forty-seven in tax under it at fifty and was glad to, because a crown that keeps its figures is worth paying. The Emperor's Share took ten off my canal and the river took two more for a ditch that never broke. It's a strange tax that fines you for being lucky, but I'll not grumble about it with water still running in my fields. I bought ten more squares up the north end of the coast, which makes twenty-four now. I let the crown off half its share both years, because I'd rather see the last works dug than see my granary fat and the purse empty. Rob's lad still takes his five off me every year like a man collecting the milk, and I've four hundred and sixty grain and not one piece on the board for him to carry off. What's left is two hundred and ten: Clark's own canal, Rich's reservoir, Reina's irrigation and the Pagoda. I mean to keep paying until every line of it is struck off. Reina's got her pen ready, and for once I'm glad of the way she counts.
