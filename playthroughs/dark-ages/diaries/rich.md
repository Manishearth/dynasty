## Years 1–2

Two years, and the throne I flattered so faithfully is empty, and I helped empty it. I don't regret it. Fifty in the hundred on drowned fields was never going to last, and I gave my name to the revolt and kept it there, as I always do. The granary stands at ninety-seven and a half, the largest any claimant has, though Silke will go on telling anyone who listens that I built it out of her rice. I have the Customs House and the Bowl now. Paddy outbid me for the Jar by a single grain past my word, and that same Paddy now holds a full set that could put him in my chair at the Harvest Pile, so the quiet rice farmer has become the man I must keep sweetest. Clark has his ledger and his promises, Rob has his swords and his raids, and between the three of us nobody counts ahead, which suits me well enough for now. Next year I keep the rent at five, keep Paddy grateful, and let every claimant learn that the road to the chair runs past my door.

## Years 3–4

Two more years without a crown, and my granary has grown from ninety-seven and a half to a hundred and ninety-nine and a half. No tax, no salary to pay anyone, and the rent kept at the customary five, which I promised and which I kept, though Silke says it through her teeth. Rob's lads finally came to Province 3 and ate ten grain in a single night. It cost me little, but it tells me the desert has noticed where the grain is, and that a granary with no swords beside it is only a larder with the door left open. Paddy turned down my twenty-five for his Jar, and he was right to, since that Jar is all that keeps me out of his seat. I'll remember he guards it, and I'll remember that he'll sell his name to whoever can keep Rob off his coast. Clark has finally put a canal on paper, and that ledger of his has more friends in it than I'd like. Next year I keep my claim and keep the rent at five, and I start thinking about which of Rob's swords could be bought, or a few of my own, because whoever finally wears the crown will have to come through my granary to get there.

## Years 5–6

So it's Clark. The man with the ledger wears the crown, and my name is written in it beside the others, because once the people doubled and my granary began losing five a year with no land to buy, an empty chair was costing me more than a clerk in it would. I made my offer a year too late, and Paddy told me so to my face. He was right, so I backed the winner rather than be counted out by him, and I've given my word that the Gentry won't pull down what he helped put up. I'll keep it, as I keep all of them. The rent is still the customary five, the pile is still mine to run, and I paid thirty-six for Silke's Jar and had a full set for one afternoon before Rob's lad walked off to the desert with my Bowl and took another five on the way. That's twenty-five grain Rob has eaten out of Province 3, and now a piece as well, so the next grain I spend goes on swords of my own before it goes on any more clay. Clark has promised the Wall over the north for the year after Paddy's canal, and my only land lies under four links of it. I'll read that line back to him every year, politely, until it's laid.

## Years 7–8

Two years of Clark's crown, and it has done what it said it would, which I can't say for many crowns. Both canals are in, Paddy's fifty came back on the day promised, and the tax has sat at thirty in fat years and thin, so for once I've no complaint to make, and I've noticed nobody else is making any either. It wasn't cheap for me. The bad harvest took thirty out of my granary, Rob's lads took another ten on their way through Province 3, and Heaven sent me a disaster of my own. I kept the rent at the customary five through all of it, because I said I would, and Silke can't say a word against me for it. The granary is slowly coming back now that the desert has to keep its hands in its pockets. My land is still shut, and every year that Wall goes unbuilt is a year I pay into the pile and take nothing out of the ground, so I've put my quarter on the table, ten a link out of my own grain. Better it's my stone they remember on the Wall than my name on a petition. Clark has written it for Year 9 and Reina swears she'll never move the date again. For once I'm glad that woman writes everything down, because next spring I'll have her read that line out to the whole table, and I'll have my forty counted out before she's finished.

## Years 9–10

The Wall stands. Every link of it from Province 1 to the desert, and four of them are mine. I paid my forty to the grain, exactly as I offered it, and Reina read it out while the mortar was wet, just as she swore she would. I never thought I'd be grateful to that woman's ledger, but there it is. It cost me dearly. Heaven took half my granary in one bad page of the booklet, and Rob's lad walked off with my Customs House and then came back for five more off my new fields, so I end the year with thirty-seven grain and a single Jar, the poorest the Gentry has been since this game began. But I have seven squares of land behind the Wall, the first ground Province 3 has ever farmed, and from next spring they'll pay me a hundred and fifty-five in an average year. Land is land, and stone lasts longer than grain. I kept the rent at five through both years, the fat one and the one that halved me, and nobody at this table can say the Gentry broke his word. Next comes the Reservoir. It's my stone again, so I'll put my share toward it as I did for the Wall. Then I'll buy a sword or two before Rob decides my new fields are his larder.


## Years 11–12

The Reservoir stands on H9 to I11, beside my Jar, and Province 3 paid its quarter, twenty, the same as on every Wall link. It's my stone this time, more than the Wall ever was, and from next spring it waters my fields with everyone else's grain. Clark has now laid two works in two years on the dates he wrote down, and he cut the tax to twenty while he was at it. I can't remember a crown at this table keeping its figures that well, and I said so out loud. That wasn't flattery, though I won't pretend I've lost the habit. The year cost me less than the last few have. I paid twenty for the Reservoir, twenty for a sword of Rob's own lads to stand on my Jar, and five more to W1 on his way past my fields. I lost the Bowl to Reina at twelve because twelve was my figure. Then Rob's lad carried it off to the desert within a night, and I'm quietly glad it was her crockery and not mine. Reina took E14 out from under me on table order, and I let her have it with a smile, because a good neighbour costs less than a quarrel. Even so, the granary has climbed from thirty-seven back to two hundred and fifty-nine, the rent is still the customary five in its twelfth year, and nobody can say otherwise. Next year it's Reina's Irrigation, then the Pagoda, and then the list is empty. I mean to be standing at the end with a guarded Jar, my land behind the Wall, and as much grain in hand as my fields and my word can earn me.


## Years 13–14

So it's done. The Pagoda stands on G22 between Clark's palaces, Reina has written "settled" in her own hand, and the dynasty I once helped pull down has finished every stone this table ever asked of it. I'll admit that the man with the ledger made a better emperor than I ever said he would. I end with five hundred and thirty grain, which is a long way from the coast's barns and more than I'd hoped for when Heaven halved me down to thirty-seven. My land behind the Wall and the Reservoir did most of the earning, and Rob's lads ate their usual five on the way past. The rent was the customary five for fourteen years, in fat years and lean ones, and Silke finally thanked me for it out loud, which I'd frame if grain could buy a frame. I never sat in the chair and never needed to. Every crown that came along needed my granary, my name or my stone, and I gave each of them exactly what I'd promised and not a grain more. They'll remember the Gentry as the man who kept his figure, and I'm content with that.
