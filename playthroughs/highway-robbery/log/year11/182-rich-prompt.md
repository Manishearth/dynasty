## Rich's diary so far, in his own words

## Setup–Year 2

Two years, and the set is whole: Silke's Jar and Paddy's Bowl for ten apiece, and Clark's own Customs House for twenty-six, bought out from under him with a smile and a thank-you. It cost me the better part of my grain, and Rob's riders took two more fives off me. Still, I end the year with fifty-five, and twenty is all a seat change needs. Clark tried to sell my squares to the Warlord, and the Warlord was vulgar enough to say so in front of everyone. I couldn't have paid for a better turn of events, and from now on I'll treat Clark with exaggerated kindness. Silke has a set too but no grain, so I must keep the rent gentle and the lady friendly until I've moved. Next comes the throne. I'll bow to Reina all the way up the steps, and I'll want somebody else's soldier between my fields and that desert when I'm sitting on it.

## Years 3–4

I said the throne came next and it did. I bowed to Reina, thanked her for the steps, and walked straight up them with her reserve in my pocket. It wasn't quite the hundred and four I'd counted on, but my pieces went back to stock before that rider on I11 could reach them, and that was worth more than the difference. I taxed at half to keep Silke's purse under twenty and put the canal in Paddy's paddies to sweeten it. It worked for exactly one auction step, and then she took Clark's seat instead of mine. I can't say I mind watching Clark fall to the coast, and I'll keep my promise of a canal to Province 5 so that he has to thank me for it. The crown holds two hundred and the palaces are untouched. But Silke has the book, eighty-five grain, a Deed and a Bowl, and the only Dragon Jar left goes on the block next year. Whoever buys that Jar decides whether I keep this chair, so I'll pay Silke her ten to the grain, smile at her through every collection, and make sure the bidding on that Jar costs her everything she has.

## Years 5–6

I wrote that whoever bought the Dragon Jar would decide whether I kept the chair, and I was right, only it went cheaper than I'd planned for. Silke paid thirty-one, I never got a bid in to make it cost her everything, and she walked up the steps with my two hundred and fifty-seven in her pocket while I was still thanking Clark for his gratitude. I kept every word I'd given: forty percent and not fifty, the canal to Province 5, ten to the Official, and in the end I'm the one keeping the lady's book. I lost the Scroll by a grain, the Jar by a grain and the Bowl by a grain, and that pattern ought to shame a man who calls himself a calculator. Reina took the chair back within the year using the same arithmetic Silke used on me, which tells me the throne goes to whoever is patient and holds a full purse, not to whoever has been kind. So I'll be patient. I have eighty-two, an empty board, and three pieces in stock next year. Clark has a Bowl and Paddy needs only a Jar, so next year I bid a grain past careful myself, collect Reina's tax to the half million, and smile at her the whole way up.

## Years 7–8

I said I'd bid a grain past careful, and I did. I took Paddy's Bowl by a grain over Silke, and it felt so good I nearly forgot to thank her. Then I put it in the far corner, told the table no boots ever went there, and Rob walked his rider up to within one step of it inside two years and fined me five each way. That's the last time I describe my own fields out loud. The Jar was never mine to win: Paddy came with a hundred and eighteen and needed only that piece. I made him pay thirty-five for it, Silke made him pay forty-six, and now he's the Gentry and needs only a Bowl, and the only Bowl on the board that would do is mine. Clark is worse. He has a hundred and seventy-two, a Bowl and a Customs House, and Paddy's Scroll comes up next year, which is the only piece Clark is missing. I have fifty-nine and still need two pieces. I can't outbid him, so I'll make the Scroll cost him every grain my purse can push, bow to Reina while I do it, and remind her that the man who wants her chair isn't the one carrying her basket.

## Years 9–10

I went into Year 9 with nine grain, so I couldn't make the Scroll cost Clark anything at all. He took it for thirty-one, completed his set and climbed straight past me into my own ledger chair, and in doing it he threw me onto the coast. I thanked him, and for once I almost meant it, because the coast he despised paid me four hundred and sixty in a single harvest, and the crown paid half of every square of sea I added to it. With that grain I finally bid past careful. I took the Customs House by one grain over Silke and the Bowl by one grain over Paddy, and that's the same single grain I'd lost by three times before, only this time it fell my way. I'm sorry about Paddy. He made me a decent offer, and my limits had already beaten him to it. Now I hold two of the three pieces, and both Land Deeds in China sit in Rob's sand. He's taken Clark's Scroll off him, and his men are walking my farm and fining me five for the privilege. I said nothing aloud about where my pieces went, and his rider still stands two steps from the Customs House, which shows how little my silence protects them. Rob doesn't sell, or so he says. But he never stops talking about prices, and a man who takes a raise from the Empress can be bought. Next year I'll keep my purse full, keep the crown's tax honest, and find out what figure finally makes the Warlord sell a piece out of his desert.
