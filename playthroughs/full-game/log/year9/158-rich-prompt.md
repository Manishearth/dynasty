## Rich's diary so far, in his own words

## Setup–Year 2

Year Two, by candlelight, from the Summer Palace. Two years ago I was a landlord with sixty grain and a smile, and tonight I'm writing at the Emperor's desk. I kept the rent at five, so the peasants thanked me for my kindness when they should have counted. I bid one grain over and never two, and I let the others feed the Harvest Pile with their pride. Clark has written my name in ink on page two of his ledger. That's fair enough, since I never promised him the Vase, and I keep my promises exactly as I make them and no wider. I've paid him eight to soften it, and I'll pay more if his ledger grows. Reina takes my old fields and my two Warlord guests with remarkable grace. I must watch that grace, because she knows exactly how this seat is won. Rob still owes the crown his tax, and now it's my crown he owes it to. The works still owed come to 950. Tomorrow I find out whether a calculating man can also build a Wall.

## Years 3–4

Year Four, from the Summer Palace, with a sore hip from that tribute horse. I've kept the tax at thirty both years and called it moderate, and nobody argued, because moderate is a word people find hard to object to. I dug two canals past my own door and put four links of Wall over my own head. The crown now harvests sixty in a good year, and the works owed are down to 710, so a gracious sovereign is also a solvent one. Graham took Clark's seat tonight with exactly the set I once used myself, and Reina told the whole table he'd played the auction the way Rich taught us. That was a compliment, and it was also a warning. Clark is in the wheat now with sixty grain and a long memory, and the ledger and its pen have gone to Graham. That means I'll have to start over on someone else's credit side, at eight a year. Reina has her Customs House and her Brush tucked under the Wall, and she has wanted my chair since the day I took hers. Noah needs only a Bowl and twenty grain, and he says so out loud, which is braver than I'd be. Rob still owes the crown nine grain, has two pieces of other men's treasure on his dunes, and has yet to look at my palace. I'll keep smiling and keep paying, and I'll keep a full granary for the day someone comes to the door with a full set.

## Years 5–6

Year Six, from the Winter Palace, which still smells of new plaster. In one evening the Official's chair went round the table three times, from Graham to Noah, Noah to Reina and Reina to Silke, and each of them walked off with the last one's purse. Meanwhile I sat on the throne and smiled, and the throne was the one seat nobody wanted. That isn't flattery, it's arithmetic: whoever takes the throne takes it with someone else's Vase two minutes behind them. I kept the tax at thirty and called it moderate again. I put a palace at H21, where no soldier's boots can reach it, and dug two canals through Paddy's fields at the crown's cost, so his harvest is twenty a year fatter and he owes me a kindness. Noah was right to correct me: a ditch pays the crown only in the year his card says so, and I'll remember that to the letter, as I remember everything. The works owed are down to 610 and the granary holds 245, and what keeps me awake is Silke. She sits on four hundred and fifty-nine grain, one Dragon Jar short of my chair, and the only Jars for sale are in Reina's cart, and Reina has every reason to remember who took her purse. Noah priced his Deed at 365 and called it a price for a throne, and I'll be paying very close attention to what Reina charges for a Jar. I'll keep smiling and keep paying Silke her eight, and every grain I don't spend is a grain she's waiting to inherit.


## Years 7–8

Year Eight, by a borrowed candle, from Province 2, where the walls are thin and nobody has plastered anything. I wrote that the throne was the one seat nobody wanted, and I wrote that every grain I didn't spend was a grain Silke was waiting to inherit, and both turned out true on the same evening. I dug eighty grain of canal through Reina's fields to keep a Jar out of Silke's cart, and Reina thanked me by selling it to her for a hundred and fifty, which was a fair price and a very poor kindness. Silke walked off with two hundred and sixty-one and a half of mine and told the table she'd keep her promises to the letter, just as she was taught. I taught her, and I can't complain about the lesson. As her collector I counted a hundred and eight into her treasury at forty percent and corrected her on my salary in front of witnesses, because eight is eight. In the auctions I bid a grain here and a grain there and won nothing, while Paddy paid fifty-one for my Customs House and took Noah's chair with it. That made me one Customs House poorer and fifty-one grain richer in the Pile than I'd planned. Clark needs only a Jar, Graham is three pieces in, and Silke sits on six hundred and twelve, so the next man through her door will be worth watching. I have a hundred and seven grain, an empty cart and a long memory, and I've started from the bottom before.
