The rules on one page

Each player runs one province of a nation whose population grows while its harvest doesn't. Public Works are the way out. Everything else is negotiated. No dice.

Goal §18

Move up. Finish in a lower-numbered province than you drew. The Emperor wins by staying on the throne, the Warlord by getting into any province. Several players can win.

Ending §18

The game ends when every work owed is built (survival), or when the table can't cope any more (collapse: nobody wins). Or agree on eight rounds beforehand.

Money §8

Grain, in millions. The Harvest Pile is the bank, run by the Gentry. Taxes go to the Emperor's reserve, which pays for works and salaries. Fractions round down to the half million.

A round is a year §5

1 I Ching card · 2 harvest · 3 rent · 4–5 tax · 6 one public work · 7 auction (the Warlord raids) · 8 soldiers move · 9 salaries. Round One starts at 3. Deals at any time, and none are binding.

Income §7

Harvest (100 / 75 / 50) + works + new land − consumption. The harvest never grows; consumption grows with population. Tax is a share of this, set by the Emperor.

Population §15

Five of the twenty I Ching cards raise it, up to ×6. Each rise makes everyone hungrier.

Realia §10

Each realia province auctions one of its own pieces a round. The seller keeps ¼; the Emperor never bids. Bought pieces go on a square in your province at once.

Climbing §11

Collect your objective's set plus 20 grain, then swap seats with someone your booklet names. They draw 60 from the Pile. Peasants must climb into the upper class before the throne; with no upper class at the table, the Silk Peasant may take it.

Public Works §12

The Emperor builds one project a round, haggling cost shares with the provinces. Canals and water works add to a province's harvest from the next round.

New land §13

Once your population has doubled: 20 a square, +20 a round, cost shared with the Emperor.

The Warlord §14

Soldiers move one square a round and raid where they land: 5 a square, 10 in Province 1, or the realia standing there. The Warlord must sell soldiers, 20 each; yours can't move until you've been raided.

Out of grain §8

Sell realia back to the Pile at 2, then go into debt and stop spending.

Revolt §16

Any player, with two others agreeing the Emperor has failed. Chaos follows until a new Emperor is acclaimed.

Personality §17

Play your booklet's character. It's a rule.

1

The game in short

The Emperor is just and benevolent, while the Official can be persuaded to take bribes. The Gentry/Landowner will try to raise the rents legally, while the lawless Warlord uses soldiers to raid the neighboring provinces — to lie, cheat, steal. Meanwhile, the Peasants can always revolt.

Your game of Dynasty simulates a real-life environment. The object is survival: survival in a somewhat hostile and competitive society. Each player is handed a province, a personality and a set of obligations, and must make a living inside a system that was not designed to be fair.

The nation must feed a growing population from a harvest that does not grow by itself. The only way out of that trap is Public Works, and Public Works need the Emperor's money and everyone's cooperation. Nearly everything else is negotiable. There are no dice. Prices, cost-sharing, bribes, alliances, threats and rents are all settled by talking, and most of the rules exist to say who has leverage over whom.

The winner or winners are those who improve their social position. The game ends in one of two ways: all of the Public Works are built, in which case the players have collectively survived and the Emperor's reign has been a glorious one; or the players cannot solve the social-economic-political problems the game presents — that is, the system has defeated their ability to cope. Section 18 sets out how to settle the result.

The map

Seven bordered provinces are the developed land. Around them lies undeveloped land: the Warlord's territory to the west and south, the strip below the Great Wall to the north, and the sea to the east. A soldier may stand on and cross all three; undeveloped land raids nothing.

Squares produce nothing. A province's harvest is the same whatever its size (Section 7). The squares hold realia, raids, soldiers and new harvest squares, and Section 13 says where each province may expand. The rivers matter for one rule: a Warlord soldier pays a toll to cross one (Section 14).

Order of play

A round is one year. Round One begins at step 3, since the harvest has already been paid out and I Ching is not consulted. The Peasants still pay rent in Round One.

  1. ScholarI Ching. Turn the top card and read it aloud. Apply any change in population, and play a Personal Disaster if the card calls for one.
  2. GentryHarvest. Pay each player the harvest the card designates, plus any increase earned by Public Works and new harvest squares in that province, less what its people consume (Section 6).
  3. PeasantsRents. Each Peasant pays the Gentry a land rental. If the Gentry does not demand it, the Peasants need not pay.
  4. EmperorTax declared. The Emperor announces a percentage, the same for everyone.
  5. OfficialTax paid. Each player calculates the tax owed and pays the Official, who delivers it to the Emperor.
  6. EmperorPublic Works. The Emperor pays for and builds one project. Cost-sharing and new land purchases are negotiated now.
  7. All, in orderAuction. Beginning with Province 1 and proceeding in numerical order, each player auctions one piece of realia. Then the Harvest Pile auctions any pieces sold back to it (Section 8).
  8. WarlordWarlord. The Warlord's soldiers move and raid (Section 14); then provincial soldiers may move one square.
  9. EmperorSalaries. The Emperor pays the Official and the Scholar.

Trading, bribery, alliances, threats and private deals may happen at any point in the round. No deal is binding. A promise is worth exactly what the player who made it is worth, and a broken one is answered at the table, not by the rules.

Note 5 No booklet gives a round order. This sequence follows the Order of Play printed on the can, with rents, salaries and soldier movement slotted in from the booklets that specify their timing. Round One skips only what the can skips, the I Ching card and the harvest; the booklets ask for rent "at the beginning of each round", Round One included.

7

Population, harvest, consumption

This is the engine of the whole game, and it is worth understanding before the first card is turned.

Your harvest does not depend on how many people you have. Whatever your population, a good harvest is 100 million bags of grain, an average harvest 75 million and a bad harvest 50 million. The Warlord's barren land yields 40, 30 and 20.

Your consumption does. Twelve million people eat 40 million bags a year, no matter how good or bad the harvest. So do fourteen million. The Emperor's court is more expensive: fourteen million people in Province 1 eat 60 million. The Warlord's eight million eat 20 million.

income = harvest + public works increases − consumption

When an I Ching card announces that the population has increased by one half or doubled, every province's population changes with it — and so does its consumption. The harvest does not. Population growth is pure loss, and the nation slides toward famine unless somebody builds something. This is the problem the game is asking you to solve.

How the population moves

Growth is national and it is uniform. The provinces start at different sizes, but a card that doubles the population doubles everyone: the base numbers differ, the multiplier never does. That is why the income tables below are indexed by multiple rather than headcount, and why a single row of one table lines up with the same row of every other.

It has a ceiling, and the ceiling is reachable. A doubling card takes effect at most twice; an increase-by-one-half card takes effect once. The nation therefore tops out at 100 × 2 × 2 × 1½ = 600 million, which the cards state outright — and which is exactly the ×6 row at the bottom of every table. Once the country is there, it is there for good, and a bad harvest at ×6 costs a peasant province 190 million bags it does not have.

The Scholar keeps the national population counter and announces the new figure whenever it changes.

The Harvest Pile

The Harvest Pile is the bank, and the Gentry runs it. It is made up of forty half-million bags, fifty 1-million bags, forty 2-million bags, fifty 5-million bags, twenty 10-million bags, twenty 20-million bags, ten 50-million bags and ten 100-million bags.

It is not the Emperor's treasury. Taxes go to the Emperor personally, into a private reserve, and that reserve pays for Public Works and salaries. The Harvest Pile is separate.

Out of the pileInto the pile
Every player's annual harvestFines and personal disasters
New harvest squares, when bought75% of every auction price

Realia sold back, at 2 million a piece
Every payment for a Public Work

The Gentry performs all transactions concerning the pile — which means the greediest player at the table is also the banker. Watch closely.

Every bid drains the empire. Three quarters of each auction price goes to the pile, so when two players bid a piece up from 10 to 30 the table is 15 poorer, grain that would have paid the tax and the Public Works. A contested piece can be settled before the auction instead: one bidder stands aside for a favor or first claim on the next.

Rounding. The smallest bag is half a million, so every payment worked out as a fraction — an auction commission, a tax, a personal disaster that takes a half or a third — rounds down to the half million, in favour of whoever pays. A tax of 30% on an income of 15 is 4.5; at 30% on 17 it is 5.1, so 5. A third of 31 is 10.

Running out of grain. If a payment would take you below zero, first sell realia back to the Harvest Pile at 2 million a piece, your choice of pieces, until it is covered. The pile auctions those pieces at the next auction, after the provinces' lots, and keeps the whole price.

Still short? You owe the rest to the pile. Until it is paid you can't bid, trade, give grain, pay toward a public work, buy soldiers or change provinces, and everything you receive pays the debt first. Whoever takes over your province takes the debt with your grain; an Emperor acclaimed in debt pays it from the reserve. Debt is a cause for revolt (Section 16), but doesn't affect scoring.

Example. The Silk Peasant holds 26 million and one Bamboo Brush when a drought brings an income of −45 million. The Brush sells back for 2, leaving 28; after the 45, the Silk Peasant owes 17, sits out this round's auction, and pays it off from the next grain in.

Taxation

The Emperor declares a tax percentage at the start of each round, from 10% to 90%. Every player then pays that percentage of their income for the year, rounded down to the half million (Section 8). The Emperor pays no tax: it would only go back into the same reserve.

Income means the harvest income from Section 7: the year's harvest, plus what your Public Works and new harvest squares add, less what your people eat. All of it is taxable. Rent, salaries, commissions, tolls, tribute and deals are not income for tax. In Round One, when no harvest is paid, each player's income is the 60 million they started with.

Note 8 The can calls it "the TAX on the Harvest"; the booklets' "All income is taxable" sits under a schedule of harvest incomes. So tax is on harvest income only. A player whose income is negative pays no tax: the schedule is income × rate and has no rows below zero.
A good Emperor will always take care of his people, and will collect an adequate tax to do so. A simple guide is: in the U.S., the middle income bracket is about 25–35%; in the United Kingdom, about 50%, due to the numerous welfare programs; and in dictatorships taxes have been as high as 80–90%. How you run your "country" is up to you! And as you know, most governments plan ahead.General Instructions, 1969

Naturally, the Emperor's decrees must be reasonable, or the players will revolt. Three of the six causes for revolt are about money the Emperor did or did not take.

The Official collects the tax and delivers it, and is also expected to make certain none of the players are cheating, which is a joke the game does not acknowledge. The Emperor may instead collect directly, or designate any player as tax collector.

The Warlord may refuse to pay. That is in character, and nothing in the rules can force the western territory to hand over grain. The Emperor's answers are the ones a real crown would have: soldiers, alliances, and a table that remembers. A Warlord who pays is buying something, and should say what.

Stock waits beside the board; bought pieces go on it immediately. Your unsold stock is not placed on the map. The moment a piece is sold, the buyer must place it on a square inside their own province, before play moves on. See Placement below.

At four or five players the second type you hold is exactly the type your own objective does not ask for, and it comes in a pair — enough for both players who need it. Your own piece is different: there is only one, and both other players holding realia need it. So in a small game only one player can complete a set at a time, and the others wait for a seat change to send pieces back to auction.

Note 9 The original rules never state this pattern, but it holds for every province. It decides the shape of every small game: the pairs sell easily, and the single pieces are the whole fight.

The auction

You must auction one piece of realia during each round, until your province's stock is gone. Realia you have bought is yours to keep: you will only need to sell the realia your province does not need to advance. The minimum bid is 2, or any higher minimum you set. A lot nobody bids on must be offered again next round at 2: you may hold a piece back one round by citing a ridiculous price, never two in a row. One fourth of the final sale price, rounded down to the half million, belongs to you as a commission; the rest goes to the Harvest Pile. Any realia returned to you during the game must be auctioned again as soon as possible. The Emperor does not bid; everyone else may.

One of the penalties of hoarding — that is, not bidding for the Realia — is that a player can and will move into or take over a wealthy Province. Remember, a player will move laterally if the reward is great enough.General Instructions, 1969

Placement

Any realia you acquire, whether at auction or by private trade, must be placed on a square within the boundaries of your Province. It cannot be moved off that square except when it is traded or sold to another player, sold back to the Harvest Pile for 2 million bags of grain, or you move to another province.

Realia sitting on a square is also a target. If the Warlord raids that square, the piece itself may be demanded as tribute.

Unsold stock stays off the map. A piece goes onto a square the moment it is bought at auction or taken in a trade — the buyer places it at once. Pieces returned on a change of province go back into their province's unsold stock until they are auctioned again.

Note 10 The booklets only say that realia you acquire is placed. Keeping unsold stock off the map stops the Warlord collecting their whole objective in Round Two, before anyone has bought anything, and it hides nothing: every seller must auction a piece each round until the stock is gone.

Objectives and changing provinces

Every province except the Emperor's and the Warlord's has an objective: a set of realia belonging to other people, plus 20 million bags of grain.

PlayersWhat you must collect
4–5Four specific pieces of realia, listed in your booklet, and 20 million bags of grain

7+One piece of every type of realia except your own, and 20 million

The Warlord's objective is different: raid a different piece of realia from each other player who holds realia. That leaves out the Emperor: the palaces are buildings, not realia. But at eight players, where the list is longest, one raid on a palace or the Pagoda may stand in for any one player's piece (Appendix G).

Becoming Emperor

When you have accumulated all of the necessary realia you may declare yourself Emperor and exchange provinces with the Emperor. You must return all realia in your possession to the Provinces where they originated, keeping only your grain surplus. The Emperor must give you the entire imperial grain reserve, then takes your former position, is entitled to all your former privileges, and draws 60 million bags of grain from the Harvest Pile.

Only the Official, the Gentry, the Scholar and the Warlord may take the throne this way. A Peasant must first move up into an upper-class province and complete that province's objective. In the peasant game, where no upper class is at the table, the Silk Peasant stands in for it: a Silk Peasant who completes the objective may take the throne.

Not during Chaos. While the throne is empty after a revolt (Section 16), it passes only by acclamation. A completed set, or the Warlord's objective, can still replace any other seat, but not take the throne.

Note 11 The booklets carry boilerplate that appears to let anyone declare themselves Emperor; the peasants' own objectives do not. The objectives govern, except for the Silk Peasant at a table with no upper class.

Replacing other players

When you have accumulated all of the realia listed under Objectives, you may instead choose to replace a player other than the Emperor. You may keep all realia you have purchased except those pieces which originated in the province you are moving into. Realia belonging to your new province must be placed on sale at the earliest possible time. If you elect to replace the Scholar, you must auction the bamboo brushes; if you elect to replace the Silk Peasant, then you must auction the dragon jars.

You may keep all of your grain surplus, as well as all grain belonging to the player you replace.

When being replaced

Return all realia in your possession to the Provinces where they originated. Change seats with the player replacing you and become the new owner of that player's old province. You may draw 60 million bags of grain from the Harvest Pile, and must now play the game according to your new personality.

Note 12 If a piece's home province is empty, it goes back to the province that started the game holding it.

A new Warlord takes over the army where it stands, since the desert's soldiers serve whoever holds the desert, and starts the objective afresh: only their own raids count.

Climbing on

A player who changes seats keeps most of a set and two players' grain, so the next move is close: usually one piece short, and everyone at the table knows which. This is deliberate. It matters most to a Peasant, who must climb twice to reach the throne, and it is what lets the lower class keep pace with an upper class that starts one step away.

The result is settled by the seat you hold when the game ends (Section 18), and finishing the Public Works ends it. A player who has climbed does well to help pay for them, before somebody else climbs over them.

13

New land and sea

Harvest squares are the only place in Dynasty where your production grows with your population. Every other income in the game is flat. They are also, deliberately, out of reach until the crisis has already started.

When you may develop

  • Your province's population must have at least doubled — 24 million for Provinces 2, 3 and 4; 28 million for Provinces 1, 5, 6 and 7. This can only happen when an I Ching card announces an increase, so the earliest possible purchase is Round Two and in practice it is much later.
  • The strip below the Great Wall opens to a province only when all four links above that province are built: for Provinces 2 and 3, that is their only new land; Province 1 may develop the sea before then.

Cost and yield

New harvest squares cost 20 million bags of grain each, purchased from the Harvest Pile during the Public Works step and marked with a green square. Each produces 20 million bags per round, for the rest of the game, starting with the next round's harvest. The cost must be shared with the Emperor, who may pay any part of it, up to the whole, and will also seek to share the harvest. Negotiate.

Buy as soon as you can, and buy ahead. The moment land opens, buy for the next population card, not this one. Breaking even in an average year takes one square at ×2, three at ×3 and nine at ×6, and the deck can jump a stage at once. A province in debt can't pay its share, so the squares you didn't buy while you could are the ones you'll need the Emperor or a neighbor to buy for you.

A rich Emperor should pay more. Every square the crown pays for raises a province's income for good, and income is what the tax is levied on. An Emperor with a full reserve does well to pay a large share, or all of it: richer provinces pay more tax, and can share the cost of the great works.

There is no limit. A province may buy as many squares as it can pay for, every round, and the purchases are not the round's one public-works project: the Emperor still builds a project as well. A square pays for itself in a single round and keeps paying, which makes new land the strongest investment in the game. That is why the population rule matters: nobody can buy a square until their province's population has doubled, so this route opens only late in the game, when the doubling has already pushed consumption past most harvests.

Where the squares go

Each province develops the ground its booklet names, touching the province or a square it has already bought: Provinces 2 and 3 the strip below the Great Wall; Provinces 4 and 5 the sea; Province 6 the sea or the Warlord's land; Province 7 the Warlord's land; Province 1 the strip or the sea. A square bought becomes part of the province. The Warlord can raid it like any other square of the province: a soldier that steps onto one raids it, in the Warlord's land or in the sea.

An Emperor who cannot or will not subsidize the development of new harvest areas is one of the six causes for revolt. The Emperor knows it, and so do you.

14

The Warlord, raids and soldiers

Since the Emperor represents law and order, the Warlord is disorder. Where and how disorder strikes is up to the Warlord.General Instructions, 1969

Raiding

The Warlord marches a soldier onto an un-raided square inside a province and demands tribute: 5 million bags of grain, or 10 million in Province 1. Place a pink raid square on that spot and stand the soldier on it. The Warlord may raid as many squares in a round as there are soldiers to do it.

Realia. If the raided square holds a piece of realia, the Warlord may take the piece itself instead of the 5 million. Remember, the Warlord needs to raid one piece of realia from every other player who holds realia to win: everyone but the Emperor. At eight players, one raid on the Summer Palace, Winter Palace or Marble Pagoda may stand in for one player's piece.

Structures. If it holds one of the Emperor's buildings — the Summer Palace, Winter Palace or Marble Pagoda — the Emperor pays the full cost of the structure, 20, and the building stays standing. A raid on any other square, including one holding the Grand Canal, the irrigation system or a Reservoir, is an ordinary raid; the work is undamaged.

Raided ground stays raided

Once a square has been raided and the Warlord paid, that square cannot be raided again, ever. The raid square remains on the map, though the Warlord's soldiers may land on a raided square as often as they wish. A province player whose own soldier occupies a raided square may reclaim it and remove the marker; it still cannot be raided a second time.

Note 14 The booklets say a raided square may be reclaimed but never say what reclaiming does. Here it removes the marker and nothing more.

Crossing water

The Warlord's soldiers may go anywhere on the board: into any province, beyond the Wall, across rivers and through the sea. A sea square raids nothing and charges no toll, and stepping ashore from it crosses nothing. Like every soldier, though, each moves only one square a round (below). Every soldier that crosses a river or a public works project costs the Warlord a transportation fee of 5 million bags of grain, paid to that province. It is the only money that reliably flows out of the western territory.

Buying soldiers

Your only defense against the Warlord is to purchase soldiers. The Warlord must sell to any player who wishes to buy, at 20 million bags of grain each, keeping 25% of each sale as commission and turning the rest over to the Harvest Pile. Arming yourself is therefore also how you fund the army coming for you.

Guarding a piece. A soldier standing on a piece of realia guards it: a Warlord soldier can take the piece only by capturing the guard straight on, and to do that it must first end a move beside the guard, where the guard can capture it. One raider can't break a guard that is free to move; two can, if one is willing to be the bait. Until your province has been raided, though, your soldiers can't move, so a guard on a piece near the border is a gift: the Warlord steps straight onto it, captures it and takes the piece in one move. Place realia deep, and guard it from inside.

The Warlord's reinforcements

At the end of the Warlord's turn, after their soldiers have moved, the Warlord may hire one new soldier for 20 million bags of grain, paid to the Harvest Pile, and stand it on any square of their own territory in the west. It moves from the next round. The army may grow to one more soldier than it started with: three at five or six players, five at eight. A Warlord in debt can't hire. This is how raids turn into power, and why a lost soldier hurts but doesn't end the campaign.

Moving soldiers

Place purchased soldiers in your own province. They cannot move at all until the Warlord has raided your province. After that, you may move all your soldiers one square each round, but only after the Warlord's soldiers have moved. Yours may cross the sea too.

Every soldier moves at most one square a round, the Warlord's included, in any direction, diagonals included. A Warlord soldier raids the square it moves onto, so each soldier raids at most one new square a round. A soldier captures by moving onto an occupied square horizontally or vertically; a soldier can never be captured diagonally. Captured soldiers are removed from play. Soldiers may be marched together as a group, and a marched group may be dispersed simultaneously at any time.

Note 15 The booklets say capture is horizontal only; the General Instructions say horizontal and vertical. Vertical capture is allowed here.
Note 16 The Warlord's booklet says both that the Warlord's soldiers may move "anywhere on the board" and "you may move all your soldiers one square during each round." Read together: no square is off limits, but every soldier moves one square a round. Raiders who could appear anywhere would make the bought soldiers — "the only defense" — useless.

I Ching and personal disasters

The Scholar turns one card at the start of every round from Round Two onward and reads it aloud. All players must obey whatever instructions are on the card. A card sets the harvest — sometimes for everyone, sometimes good for some provinces and bad for others — and may raise the population, tax the provinces, damage unfinished Public Works, or call for Personal Disasters.

There are twenty cards and they are not reshuffled. Five of them raise the population — three that double it and two that add half — and the deck stops counting at 600 million. That is the clock the whole game runs on.

Personal disasters

Every booklet contains three, numbered 1 to 3. When I Ching announces a personal disaster, you play yours in numerical order — not the one you would prefer. When all three have been played, they may be played over again. They are printed with each province in Part Two.

The first disaster in every booklet is cheap and fixed. The third, in almost every booklet, takes a fraction of everything you have, rounded down to the half million (Section 8).

16

Revolt and Chaos

You may revolt against the Emperor at any time during the game, provided that two other players agree that one of the following exists:

  1. The Emperor is unjust.
  2. The Emperor consistently demands taxes which are too excessive.
  3. The Emperor has too low a grain reserve.
  4. The Emperor has delayed too long in building Public Works.
  5. The Emperor cannot control the Warlord.
  6. The Emperor cannot, or will not, subsidize the development of new harvest areas.

Debt is a seventh cause. A player in debt to the Harvest Pile (Section 8) is facing starvation, and starvation is the one thing the peasants' booklets say will drive them to revolt. So while any player is in debt, that debt is a cause for revolt like the six above. It still needs two other players to agree, and the player in debt may be the one who revolts.

Once a revolt is called and two others agree, play stops until it is settled. The Emperor may answer it: pay off a debt, lower the tax, fund new land, or promise anything at all. Everyone may argue, persuade and make deals. Then the player who called it and each player backing it either stands by the revolt or takes it back. It stands only if the caller and at least two others still back it; otherwise it is dropped and play goes on. Until then no step of the round happens and no seat changes hands, not even a completed set taking the throne.

The price of revolt is Chaos. The nation is leaderless until a new Emperor emerges. To become the new Emperor, a player must have one of the following: the most money, the most friends, the most soldiers, or any combination of the above. Until then the throne passes only by acclamation: no completed set and no Warlord's objective can take it (Section 11).

The sealed treasury

At the revolt the imperial grain reserve is sealed. Nobody may spend it on anything voluntary: no deals, gifts, bribes or soldiers. Province 1 still lives on it, though. Its harvest income, positive or negative, goes into or out of the treasury, and so do raids on Province 1, the Emperor's personal disasters, and any card that pays or charges "the Emperor": the chest is paid out of it, and the share and utility cards pay into it. With no tax coming in, Province 1's own consumption drains it year by year, where the whole table can count it.

The deposed Emperor has no purse. They stay in Province 1, may back a claimant and make promises, but can't claim the throne. At the acclamation the new Emperor takes the throne just as in Becoming Emperor (Section 11), but needs no set and no 20 million in hand: they return all their realia to the provinces it came from and keep their own grain, they take the whole treasury, even if it has run below zero, and the deposed Emperor takes their old seat and draws 60 million bags of grain from the Harvest Pile.

Counting the acclamation

The rules list the qualifications but not how to choose between rival claims, so count them. Anyone but the deposed Emperor may claim, the Warlord included. For each claimant, count three things: grain in hand; soldiers on the board that the claimant commands, which for the Warlord is their army and for anyone else the soldiers bought for their province, even ones that can't move yet; and friends, the other players backing that claim (a claimant isn't their own friend). Each player backs one claim at most. The deposed Emperor may back a claimant, but their grain isn't counted, and soldiers bought for Province 1 count for nobody.

  • A claimant who leads in one count is acclaimed, provided no rival leads in another.
  • Otherwise, the claimant who leads in more of the three is acclaimed.
  • A tie in a count means nobody leads it. If no one is acclaimed, Chaos goes on, and the table may count again whenever the claims change.

The Warlord's army usually gives them the most soldiers, so their claim blocks others more often than it wins. The Warlord must sell soldiers to anyone who asks, and buying them is how the rest of the table takes that count from them.

Note 17 While Chaos lasts, no tax is declared, no salaries are paid and no Public Works are built, and the treasury is sealed. Harvests are still distributed and the Warlord still rides.

Note the second qualification in that list. Friends are a resource in this game, and the rules say so out loud.

Playing your personality

Each booklet opens with a personality, and this is a rule, not flavor text. For as long as you hold a province, your actions must be consistent with your personality as stated. A generous Official is not playing the game. A Gentry who forgets to collect the rents is not playing the game.

The revolt rules are the enforcement mechanism. There is no referee except the Emperor, and no penalty except the one the table agrees to impose.

Note that although the Peasants are described as not overly ambitious, they are ambitious enough to want to move ahead, up the social ladder.

Being sketchy is allowed. Deals are not binding (Section 5), and you may keep your grain out of sight and be as cagey about it as your personality allows: say what you hold, round it, or lie. The Scholar always keeps promises; the Warlord never has to. The rules only count grain when it changes hands.

Players should negotiate with each other, help each other (if you so desire), hurt each other (if so inclined), bribe the official, develop special strategy against the Warlord, find ways to combat overpopulation, create your own "Domino Theory," or deficit spending program, a surtax. The Emperor might decree a "New Deal" or that there will be no more population increases, if this seems to be practical in terms of the real world.

This game gives you the opportunity to operate a mini country. You will be faced with all of the problems that confront most countries. How you solve the problems is the essence of the game. Use your common sense and your knowledge of the world around you. That is the true guide to Dynasty.General Instructions, 1969
18

Winning the game

The winner or winners are those who improve their social position. Note the plural: Dynasty does not crown a single victor, and it is perfectly possible for four people to win and two to lose.

How the game ends

By survival. All of the Public Works are built: three imperial buildings, twelve links of the Great Wall, eight sections of the Grand Canal, and the irrigation system and the two Reservoirs, in every province that has a player. The Emperor's reign has been a glorious one, and the nation has fed itself.

By collapse. The players cannot solve the social-economic-political problems presented by the game — the system has defeated their ability to cope. There is no procedure for this and no scoring; the table simply reaches a point where the harvest cannot cover consumption and no one can or will build. Nobody wins, except perhaps the Warlord, if they have wreaked havoc and gathered a lot of resources.

At an agreed time. The table may agree before setup to stop after a set number of rounds, and score from wherever everyone is sitting. Eight to twelve rounds is a good length (Note 19).

Settling the result

Rank every player by the number of the province they are sitting in when the game ends, 1 being highest, and compare it with the province they drew at setup.

Province 8 — The Warlord

12345678

Undeveloped land · population 8 million · consumes 20 million · harvest 40 / 30 / 20

The Warlord plays with 5, 6, or 8-or-more players, and does not appear in the four-player or seven-player game.

Personality

You are a cruel, vicious villain. You will lie and cheat and most of all, you will steal. Your only desire is to accumulate wealth enough to buy more soldiers who can defeat the Imperial Army. Then you will be Emperor. You survive by raiding the neighboring provinces and stealing whatever grain or property you can find and you will not hesitate to kill anyone who stands in your way.

Objective

Raid a different piece of realia from each other player who holds realia — everyone but the Emperor. At eight players, one raid on the Summer Palace, Winter Palace or Marble Pagoda may stand in for one player's piece. When you have done so, you may change places with any player in the game — including the Emperor.

Your pieces

Eight soldiers of one color and eight of another. Pick one color for your soldiers; the other color is sold as mercenaries. Add soldiers if necessary. Forty raid squares.

Your army

You begin with two soldiers at five or six players, four at eight or more. Your soldiers may begin the game on any square in any undeveloped land, the sea included. At the end of each of your turns you may hire one more for 20, paid to the Harvest Pile, on a square of your own territory; your army may grow to one more than you started with.

Raiding

Your major source of revenue will be from raiding the provinces. March your soldiers into a province and demand 5 million bags of grain — 10 million in Province 1 — each time a soldier lands on an unraided square. As soon as your soldier lands on a square, place a raid square on the board and stand your soldier on it.

Once a square has been raided, that square cannot be raided again. However, you may march your soldiers on a raided square as often as you wish.

If you raid a square where a piece of realia has been placed, you may take the realia as tribute instead of the 5 million.

You may move your soldiers anywhere on the board. If you cross a river or any public works, you must pay the Province player a transportation fee of 5 million for each soldier you move across the water. You may move all your soldiers one square during each round, and may raid as many squares as you have soldiers. (Note 16)

Selling soldiers

You must sell soldiers to any player who wishes to buy them. The price is 20 million per soldier, and you may keep 25% of each sale as a commission. All other grain goes to the Harvest Pile. The only defense against your army is for other players to purchase soldiers of their own — every soldier purchased must begin play in that player's Province and cannot be moved until you have raided it.

Personal disasters

  1. You were wounded in the legs in your last battle. Therefore you are unable to raid Provinces 5, 6, 7 for one round.
  2. One of your most trusted lieutenants stole your daughter and ran away to form a little band of raiders. At first you are very angry and ready to take reprisals. But on second thought, you are happy to see your daughter married off to an ambitious rival. No raids this year, you are too busy making feasts for the newlyweds. Cost: 5 million to the Harvest Pile.
  3. You received a concussion in a riding accident. After you came out of your coma, you discovered that half of your wealth has been stolen by members of your own group. Too late now to go after them. Deposit one-half of your grain in the Harvest Pile.

Part One · Before Round One

1Set up while you talk §4 · App. A

  • Pick the provinces for your player count (table at the end). Shuffle those booklets face down; each player draws one.
  • Everyone gets 60 million. That is Round One's harvest.
  • Each province takes its starting realia and keeps it beside the board, not on it.
  • The Warlord places their army (2 soldiers at 5–6 players, 4 at 8) anywhere on undeveloped land, the sea included. The other colour is stock for sale.
  • The Gentry sets out the Harvest Pile; the Scholar shuffles the I Ching face down. Make the paper cut-outs for the water works owed at this table.

2The pitch §1 · §18

  • The object is survival. The population will grow; the harvest won't. Public Works are the only way out, and they need the Emperor's money and everyone's cooperation.
  • Winning means moving up: finishing in a lower-numbered province than you drew. Several people can win at once.
  • The game ends when every public work owed is built, or when the table can't cope any more.
  • No dice. Prices, bribes, cost shares, rents and alliances are all settled by talking, at any time.
  • No deal is binding, and you may keep your grain out of sight and be cagey about it. §5 · §17
  • Your personality is a rule. Play your booklet's character: a generous Official or a Gentry who forgets the rent isn't playing the game. §17

3Grain and the bank §8

  • All money is grain, counted in millions. The smallest bag is half a million.
  • The Harvest Pile is the bank, run by the Gentry. It pays harvests and takes fines.
  • The Pile is not the Emperor's money. Tax goes into the Emperor's own reserve, which pays for Public Works and salaries.
  • Any fraction (tax, commission, a half or a third) rounds down to the half million, in the payer's favour.

4A round is a year §5

  1. ScholarI Ching. Turn a card, read it, apply population changes and personal disasters.
  2. GentryHarvest. Pay everyone from the Pile, plus Public Works bonuses.
  3. PeasantsRent to the Gentry, at least 5, but only if the Gentry asks.
  4. EmperorTax declared: one rate, 10–90%, the same for everyone.
  5. OfficialTax paid on this year's income, to the Emperor. Negative income pays nothing.
  6. EmperorPublic Works: build one project. Cost shares and land purchases are negotiated now.
  7. All, in orderAuction: each province sells one realia. The Warlord raids instead.
  8. ProvincesSoldiers: after the Warlord's, bought soldiers move one square.
  9. EmperorSalaries: at least 5 each to the Official and the Scholar.
  • Round One starts at step 3. No card is turned and no harvest is paid: the 60 everyone holds was the harvest. Rent is still due.

5Income and tax §7 · §9

  • Income = harvest + Public Works bonuses + new land − consumption. Read it off the table in your booklet.
  • The harvest is fixed: 100 / 75 / 50 for good, average and bad (the Warlord 40 / 30 / 20). The size of your province on the map changes nothing.
  • Consumption grows with population. When a card doubles the population, everyone's consumption doubles and nobody's harvest does.
  • Tax is a share of that income, and nothing else: rent, salaries, commissions and tolls aren't taxed. In Round One, income is the 60 you hold. The Emperor pays no tax, and the Warlord may refuse to.
  • New land is the way to prosperity. Each new harvest square costs 20 and adds 20 to your harvest every round after, cost and yield shared with the Emperor. But it only opens once the population has doubled (×2). §13
Worked example The Rice Peasant has one canal section built and the population stands at ×1½. A good harvest: 100 + 10 − 60 = 50. At a tax of 30% the Rice Peasant pays 15. Two years later the population has doubled and the harvest is bad: 50 + 10 − 80 = −20. The province loses 20 and pays no tax.

6Realia: how you climb §10 · §11

  • Each upper-class and peasant province makes one kind of realia and must auction one piece every round until its stock is gone. Minimum bid 2; an unsold lot reopens at 2 next round.
  • The seller keeps a quarter of the price; the rest goes to the Pile. The Emperor never bids.
  • A bought or traded piece goes onto a square in the buyer's province at once, and stays there. On the board it can be raided.
  • Your objective is a set of other people's realia plus 20 in grain. Your booklet lists it. Complete it and you may swap seats with someone your booklet names (Part 13).
  • A Peasant can't become Emperor directly. Climb into an upper-class seat first, then complete that seat's set.
  • At 4–5 players you also hold a pair of a realia you'll never need. Sell it: someone else does need it.

Part Two · When it first happens

8Before Round Two's card: I Ching §15

  • The card sets each province's harvest, and may raise the population, levy a charge, damage works or call for personal disasters. Everyone obeys it.
  • Five of the twenty cards raise the population (three double it, two add half), up to ×6. The deck is never reshuffled: it is the game's clock.
  • When a card calls a personal disaster, play your booklet's next one, in order 1 → 2 → 3, then start again. No choosing.

9The Emperor's first project: Public Works §12

  • The Emperor builds one project a round. Several sections of the same work count as one project.
  • There are a lot of projects. Your reference card lists the ones that matter to you, and who pays for each; where the crown doesn't pay alone, the Emperor and the province haggle.
  • Some give their province grain every year, from the next round on. The Great Wall opens the land to the north for new harvest squares. And some must be built before others.
  • Building every work owed at this table ends the game. Works in empty provinces aren't owed.
  • It's in most players' interest that the works get built: they raise incomes, and each one brings the end closer. If you've climbed, paying toward a work helps lock in your progress.

10The first raid: the Warlord and soldiers §14

Skip at 4 and 7 players: there is no Warlord.

  • Every soldier, the Warlord's too, moves one square a round, diagonals allowed. The sea is open to every soldier.
  • A Warlord soldier stepping onto an un-raided province square raids it: 5 tribute, 10 in Province 1. That square can never be raided again.
  • Raiding realia: the Warlord takes 5 or the piece. Raiding a palace or the Pagoda: the Emperor pays its full cost, 20, and the building stays.
  • Crossing a river or a built public work costs the Warlord 5 a soldier, paid to that province.
  • The Warlord must sell soldiers to anyone, at any time, at 20 each, keeping a quarter of the price.
  • A bought soldier can't move until the Warlord has raided your province, and then moves after the Warlord's. Capture is straight only, never diagonal.
  • The Warlord's objective: raid a different piece of realia from each other player who holds realia (not the Emperor; at eight players one palace raid may stand in for one of them). Then they may take any seat, the throne included. The Warlord may hire one soldier a round for 20, up to one more than they started with.

11The first player who can't pay §8 · Note 7

  • First sell realia back to the Pile at 2 a piece, your choice of pieces. Still short, and you're in debt to the Pile.
  • In debt you can't bid, buy, trade, give grain, pay toward works, buy soldiers or change seats. Everything you receive pays the debt first.
  • Debt is a cause for revolt (Part 14).

12The first doubling card: new harvest squares §13

  • Locked until your population has doubled. The strip below the Wall also needs all four links above your province (1, 2 and 3).
  • Then buy any number, any round: 20 each from the Pile, +20 every round after. Share the cost with the Emperor, who will want a share of the harvest.
  • Buy as soon as you can, and buy ahead of the next card: one square breaks even at ×2, three at ×3, nine at ×6. In debt you can't pay your share.
  • The Emperor may pay any part of a square, even all of it, and a rich Emperor should: richer provinces pay more tax toward the great works.

13Someone completes a set: changing seats §11 · App. E

  • Show the set and 20 grain, and name the seat you're taking: the throne (from the Official, Gentry, Scholar or Warlord) or a seat your booklet names.
  • Taking the throne, you return all your realia and take the whole imperial reserve. Taking another seat, you keep your realia except your new province's own, and take the other player's grain.
  • The player replaced returns all their realia, draws 60 from the Pile and plays the new character from now on. Returned pieces are auctioned again.

14The first angry player: revolt and Chaos §16

  • Anyone may revolt at any time if two other players agree the Emperor is guilty of one of the causes: unjust, taxes too high, reserve too low, works too slow, can't control the Warlord, won't fund new land. Or any player is in debt.
  • Once called and backed, play stops: the Emperor may answer, everyone may talk and deal, and then the caller and backers stand by it or take it back. It needs the caller and two others still behind it.
  • Revolt brings Chaos: no tax, salaries or building until the table acclaims a new Emperor, and no set can take the throne. The treasury is sealed: nobody may spend it, Province 1's harvest still runs through it, and the new Emperor inherits it, debt and all. Count each claimant's grain, the soldiers they command and their friends (backers, not counting themselves): leading one count is enough if no rival leads another; otherwise whoever leads more counts wins, and a tie leads nobody. Harvests still come, and the Warlord still rides.
  • This is how the table polices the Emperor. There's no other referee.

15The last round: settling the result §18

  • Survival: every work owed is built. Or collapse: the table can't cope any more, and nobody wins.
  • Compare your seat now with the seat you drew. A lower number is a win, the same is survival, a higher number is a loss. An Emperor still on the throne wins; a Warlord who got into any province wins.
  • Ties go to the bigger grain pile. A short game ends after eight rounds, scored the same way.

This is the "climbing on" of Section 11: everyone at the table can see what comes next.

Then the throne

A round later the Scholar buys the Wooden Bowl and holds 70 in grain. The set is complete again, and the Scholar may take the throne.

  1. ScholarReturn all realia. Taking the throne keeps none of it. Each of the five pieces goes back to its home province's unsold stock, to be auctioned again: the Customs House to Province 2, the Scroll to 3, the Jar to 5, the Bowl to 6 and the Vase to 7.
  2. EmperorHand over the reserve. The whole imperial reserve, say 112, goes to the new Emperor, who now holds 70 + 112 = 182.
  3. EmperorStep down. The old Emperor takes the Scholar's seat, booklet and jobs, draws 60 from the Harvest Pile, and must auction whatever is left of the Scholar's unsold Brushes.

The new Emperor now owes every public work still unbuilt, and the old one is a Scholar with 60 and a long way back up.

If a piece has no home In a smaller game a returned piece may belong to a province nobody is playing. It goes instead to the province that started the game holding it (Note 12).

Appendix F

F

Editor's notes

The 1969 edition scattered its rules across eight booklets, a sheet of General Instructions and the printing on the can, and the three do not always agree. Every ruling below is a decision made in reconstructing this text. The original wording is given so you can overrule it at your own table. The smaller rulings, which follow from the rules rather than overriding them, are in Appendix G.

Note 1 · Province numbering on the can

The can lists "Province 5 — Warlord" and "6, 7, 8 — Peasants," and its four-player alternate group reads "1, 6, 7, 8 — Peasant." The map and all eight booklets number the peasants 5, 6, 7 and place the Warlord in the undeveloped land as Province 8. The booklets and map win. The can preserves an earlier numbering; the player-count table has been renumbered.

Note 2 · The Warlord sits out the seven-player game

This looks like an error on the can until you check the Warlord's booklet, which lists starting armies for "5 or 6 Players" and "8 or More Players" and says nothing about seven. Two independent sources agree, so it stands.

Note 3 · Missing officers at low player counts

Three provinces carry administrative work: the Official collects tax, the Gentry banks the Harvest Pile and collects rent, the Scholar reads I Ching and counts the population. At six players there is no Scholar; in the four-player peasant game there is no Official, Gentry or Scholar at all. The rules never mention this.

  • No Official — the Emperor collects directly or designates any player as tax collector. The can already allows this.
  • No Scholar — the Emperor designates a player to turn the I Ching card and keep the population counter, and pays that player only what the Emperor chooses: an appointee is owed no salary.
  • No Gentry — the Emperor designates a player to bank the Harvest Pile and pay out harvests. No land rental is collected by anyone; there is no landlord.

Handing a job to a player who was not born to it is not neutral. An Official who also banks the Pile is a very different Official, and the table should expect that.

Note 4 · Unoccupied provinces

The Emperor's booklet says the crown need not build in a province with no player. Extended: realia belonging to an unoccupied province stays in the box and is never auctioned, and no rent is owed for an empty peasant province.

Note 5 · Round order

No booklet gives one. Section 5 follows the Order of Play printed on the can, with rents, salaries and soldier movement slotted in from the booklets that specify their timing — "at the beginning of each round" for rents, "at the end of each round" for salaries, "only after the Warlord moves his soldiers" for provincial troops. For Round One the can says only that the 60 million each player starts with "represents a 'good' harvest for the first ROUND of play" and that "I CHING is NOT consulted during the first ROUND." Round One skips the card and the harvest and starts at rent, which the Peasants' booklets require "at the beginning of EACH ROUND."

Note 6 · Consumption at the bottom of the income tables

Every printed table's population column runs ×1, ×1½, ×2, ×3, ×4 — then jumps. The 12-million provinces end at 72, which is ×6 and consistent. The 14-million provinces end at 112, which is ×8, but with consumption of 240, which is ×6; the Emperor's ends at 112 with consumption 360, also ×6. Consumption is always proportional to population. The tables in Section 7 are indexed by multiple rather than headcount, which removes the discrepancy. The I Ching cards settle it independently: they cap the nation at 600 million, which is ×6 of the starting 100 million, so the ×8 figures could never have been reached.

Note 7 · Running out of grain

No booklet, the General Instructions or the can says what happens when a payment would take a player's grain below zero; Note 8 covers only tax on a negative income. The nearest hints are the Rice Peasant's fire, "So that your family can survive for one year, you must borrow grain"; the General Instructions' suggestion that players "create your own 'Domino Theory,' or deficit spending program, a surtax"; and the peasants, who "will revolt against the Emperor only if your life is threatened, only if the alternative is death or starvation." So borrowing exists, and running out is meant to hurt. Sell before you sink: a player who owes more than they hold first sells realia back to the Harvest Pile at 2 million a piece, their choice of pieces, until the payment is covered or nothing is left; only then does grain go below zero, as a debt to the pile. A player in debt may not bid, buy, trade, give grain, pay toward a public work, buy soldiers or change provinces, and all grain received pays the debt first. Pieces sold back join the pile's own stock, and the pile auctions them at the next auction, after the provinces' lots, keeping the whole price. A bid must be covered by grain in hand, so buying can never put a player in debt. A player who takes over a province takes over the displaced player's grain, debt included; a player the table acclaims Emperor during Chaos may take the throne in debt, and the imperial reserve pays it off. Scoring is unchanged. A player in debt counts as facing starvation, which the peasants' booklets name as the one reason they revolt, so while any player is in debt the debt is a seventh cause for revolt (Section 16), still needing two other players to agree; the player in debt may be the one who revolts. A table that prefers the old free-and-easy reading can let grain go negative with no consequence; the game still works, but debt stops mattering.

Note 8 · What tax is charged on

The can's Order of Play opens with "the Emperor begins each ROUND by declaring the TAX on the Harvest." The booklets' Income Tax Schedule runs from an income of 5 to 140 and ends "Note: All income is taxable." Read together, the note says every harvest income is taxed, however small, not that rent, salaries, commissions and tolls are. Tax is charged on harvest income: the harvest, plus Public Works and new harvest squares, less consumption. In Round One that is the 60 million each player starts with, which the can calls the first harvest. Taxing every grain movement would mean keeping a ledger of each player's receipts for the year, which nothing in the game asks anyone to do. A negative income is not addressed anywhere. A player with negative income pays no tax. The schedule is income × rate and has no rows below zero. The Emperor pays no tax, since it would go straight back into the imperial reserve.

Note 9 · The realia you never need

Not a ruling but a pattern, and the most elegant thing in the design. At 4–5 players each province begins holding two pieces of one other province's realia, and in every case it is exactly the type that province's objective does not require. Provinces 2 and 5 are locked in a mutual trade; 3, 6, 4 and 7 form a ring. Because those pieces come in pairs, every player can get the one they need from the pair. Each province's own piece, though, exists once and is wanted by both other players — so in a four- or five-player game only one player can complete a set at a time.

Note 10 · Unsold stock and the map

Every booklet says: "Any realia which you acquire either during a public auction or by trading with another player must be placed on a square within the boundaries of your Province." Nothing says where a seller's unsold stock sits, and the Warlord's booklet lets them demand realia only from "a square where a piece of realia has been placed." Unsold stock stays off the map; a piece is placed the moment it is bought or traded, and returned pieces rejoin their province's unsold stock. Putting stock on the map at setup would let the Warlord raid most of their objective in Round Two and strip one-of-a-kind pieces out of a small game before they could be sold. Nothing can be hidden this way, because a seller must auction one piece every round until the stock is empty.

Note 11 · Peasants and the throne

Every booklet carries the same boilerplate paragraph headed Becoming Emperor, but the peasants' own Objectives sections name only the Gentry, Scholar, Official and other Peasants as targets — never the Emperor. The Objectives section governs. A Peasant must climb, taking an upper-class province first and completing its objective. This preserves the social ladder the General Instructions describe, and it makes the Warlord the only outsider who can seize power in a single move. The exception is the peasant game, the four- or five-player table with no upper class. There a climbing Peasant has no upper-class seat to take, so without one the realia would only shuffle the peasants among themselves, and the Silk Peasant's set would lead nowhere at all. So at that table the Silk Peasant stands in for the upper class, a petty bourgeoisie of silk, and may take the throne. The ladder keeps its pace: the Wheat or Rice Peasant climbs to Silk, then from Silk to the throne, two climbs as in the full game, and the Emperor sees each one coming.

Note 12 · Returned pieces with no home

When a player is replaced, they "return all realia in your possession to the Provinces where they originated." In a small game, some of those pieces are native to provinces nobody is playing — a peasant holding a Customs House or a Land Deed Scroll, say — and returning them to an empty province would take them out of the game. A returned piece whose home province is empty goes to the province that started the game holding it, and is auctioned from there as soon as possible.

Note 13 · One project per round

The can says the Emperor "pays for and builds one Public Works Project." The Emperor's booklet says "any number of sections may be built in any one round" of the Wall and Canal. One project per round, but a project may be any number of sections of the same work.

Note 14 · Reclaiming a raided square

Four booklets say "You may reclaim a raided square if one of your soldiers occupies the square," without saying what reclaiming does. Remove the raid marker. The square still cannot be raided a second time — that rule is absolute — but the ground is yours again and may hold realia or new development.

Note 15 · Capturing soldiers

The booklets say soldiers "can only capture horizontally, never diagonally." The General Instructions say "Soldiers cannot be captured diagonally, only horizontally and vertically." The General Instructions win: capture orthogonally in any of the four directions. Movement remains in any direction, diagonals included.

Note 16 · How far the Warlord's soldiers move

The Warlord's booklet says "You may move your soldiers anywhere on the board," and two sentences later, "You may move all your soldiers one square during each round, and may raid as many squares as you have soldiers." The General Instructions and the other booklets give purchased soldiers the same one-square move. "Anywhere" means no part of the board is off limits; "one square" is the speed. Every soldier, the Warlord's included, moves at most one square a round, and pays the transportation fee for each river or public work it crosses. The rest of the soldier rules — capture, blocking, marching in groups, and buying soldiers as "the only defense against your army" — only work if raiders have to walk. On the map this makes the frontier provinces, 7 and 3, the exposed ones; Provinces 1, 4, 5 and 6 are reached along the sea, which every soldier may cross (Appendix G).

Note 17 · What Chaos costs

The rules say the nation is leaderless until a new Emperor emerges, without saying what leaderless means. While Chaos lasts, no tax is declared, no salaries are paid and no Public Works are built. Harvests are still distributed and the Warlord still rides. This gives the revolt a real price, which is what the word is doing there.

The treasury is sealed at the revolt (Section 16), because a deposed Emperor who knows the reserve will pass to a rival would otherwise spend it on friends: use it or lose it would make emptying the treasury the best play in any Chaos. Sealed, the reserve is out of everyone's reach, and Province 1's consumption, with no tax coming in, drains it where the table can see. Chaos costs the next Emperor something everyone can count. Giving the deposed Emperor a purse of their own alongside it was considered and rejected, as two purses for one seat.

Note 18 · Scoring at the end

The can says the winner or winners are those who improve their social position, and the General Instructions say the object is survival. Neither gives a procedure. Rank by final province number against starting province number, as set out in Section 18, with grain surplus as the tiebreak. An Emperor still on the throne has won, since that is the Emperor's objective, and so has a Warlord who has entered the social order at all. Both sources treat finishing where you began as a success rather than a defeat, and this scoring keeps that.

Note 19 · A game of agreed length

The original has no turn limit: it ends when the Public Works are finished or when the players stop coping. Both endings can take a very long day. A table may agree before setup to stop after a set number of rounds, eight to twelve, and score from where everyone is sitting. This is an addition, not a reading of the rules — but it changes nothing inside the game, and Section 18's scoring already works from any stopping point, since it compares each player's final province with the one they drew.

Note 20 · The Scholar's realia at six players

Booklet 4 lists the Scholar's own realia for 4–5 players and for 7 or more, but the six-player line is missing from the printing. Every other province holds two of its own type at six. The Scholar starts with two Bamboo Brushes.

Bought soldiers after a seat changeA soldier stays with the seat, like the Warlord's army: whoever now sits in the province it was bought for commands it. It still can't move until that province has been raided.
